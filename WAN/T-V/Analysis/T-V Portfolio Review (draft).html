2 · The defined-benefit plan

$737,594 in buffered index segments — protected on the way down, capped on the way up

The DB plan does not own stocks or bonds. It owns 129 "segments" of an Equitable index-linked annuity: each tracks an index (S&P 500 45%, Russell 2000 24%, MSCI EAFE 19%, NASDAQ 100 9%, MSCI EM 3%) for one or six years, absorbs the first 10–30% of any loss, and gives up everything above a cap. Every contribution since 2021 appears to have opened a new segment — 30 started in 2026 alone — which is why the balance is spread across so many small pieces. There is no cash, bond or fixed sleeve at all: the allocation on file for new money is the money-market option, but maturing segments are instructed to roll straight into a new segment of the same type.

The statement shows what the caps have cost. Across the 25 segment-years for which it prints both numbers, the underlying index returned 10.2% a year on average and the segments were credited 7.5%. The cap bit in 10 of 25 years — Russell 2000 +47.0% became +10%, S&P 500 +29.8% became +15.5% — while the buffer helped in 5 down years, turning a −24.8% Russell year into −14.8%. Net of both, the annuity gave up roughly 2.8 points a year against the price index, and index figures exclude dividends — another 1–2 points a year segment holders never receive. The newest S&P annual-lock segments carry caps of only 7.0% and 5.5%.

That is the trade the client bought: smoother returns for a lower expected return, with no line-item fee. Whether it is worth it depends on how much downside protection a DB plan with a roughly ten-year horizon needs. On the statement's own evidence the cost has run at 2–4 points a year in a strong market — on $737,594, something like $18k–$30k a year.

Timing matters here. The contract's withdrawal charge is 3% until 9 Feb 2027 (≈ $22,128 on today's value) and 0% after; taking a six-year segment before its maturity date means accepting the interim value, on which the buffer does not apply. $399,634 of segments mature in 2026–27 and can move cleanly; the $461,512 in six-year segments is a hold-or-haircut decision to make segment by segment. The plan's actuary should confirm the funding target before any money leaves the annuity.
3 · Allocation

Almost entirely equity-linked once the DB plan is counted

Looking through the funds, the household is roughly 21% equity funds and stocks / 58% buffered index segments / 11% bonds / 10% cash and guaranteed. The buffered segments are equity exposure with a floor and a ceiling, so about 79% of the household's money moves with stock indexes. Outside the DB plan the "cash" is doing two very different jobs. Inside the 401(k), $119,053 (31%) sits in the Guaranteed Interest Option at 2.00% — appropriate as a stable-value sleeve for someone near retirement, but Tommy is in a 2035 target-date fund, which implies roughly nine years to retirement, and the GIO rate is well below what a money-market fund or T-bills pay today (about 3.7–3.8%). Outside the plan, $9,480 is parked in bank sweeps at 0.01% — most of it the $6,419 that is 43% of Sadie's growth-objective Roth IRA.

The equity sleeve is sensibly diversified where it is held in funds: the T. Rowe Price target-date funds are global multi-asset portfolios (75.8% stocks / 24.0% bonds for the 2035 fund), the kids own ACWI, and the 529 is age-based. The two concentrated spots are both in the joint account: one Treasury STRIP is 79% of it and SpaceX is 20%. The STRIP itself is fine — a AAA zero maturing in three years at about a 4.2% yield to maturity — but it pays nothing along the way, which is why an "income" account shows $0 income.

4 · Performance

The funds have done their job; the wrapper has not

Tommy's personal rate of return was 6.59% for Q2 and 6.14% for the first half of 2026; Emma's, 100% in the 2040 fund, was 11.02% and 9.89%. The gap is the GIO: the T. Rowe Price 2035 R fund itself returned 8.80% YTD and 17.32% over one year, but a third of Tommy's balance earned 2% annualized. Over ten years the 2035 fund has compounded at 9.98% a year — respectable for a 75/25 portfolio, and in line with the plan's own S&P 500 index option minus the bond drag.

Those are the fund's returns before Equitable's charges. The statement footnote says the numbers exclude the separate-account charge, contract-level charge, contingent withdrawal charge and administrative charge; only the "asset charge" ($336 for the quarter on Tommy's account) is itemized. The joint account was flat for the year (+$52 through June) because it was mostly cash and a zero-coupon bond until the SpaceX purchase; the kids' Schwab accounts returned 3–9% YTD, and the 529 gained 7.5%.

5 · Cost

About 1.7% a year, most of it invisible

Only one fee appears on the 401(k) statements: the asset charge, $352 for the quarter across both participants, or about $1,407 a year (0.53% of the variable-fund balance; the GIO is not charged). The larger cost is inside the fund. The R share class of the T. Rowe Price Retirement funds carries a 1.08% expense ratio — including a 0.50% 12b-1 distribution fee that compensates the selling representative — and is netted out of the unit value, so it never shows up as a line. On $265,742 that is roughly $2,870 a year. Equitable's own performance note lists further separate-account and contract-level charges that are not itemized anywhere in these pages.

Add the opportunity cost of the GIO (about $2,024 a year versus a 3.7% money fund) and the idle sweep cash ($350), and the household is giving up roughly $6,650 a year — 1.73% of the plan. The same balances at Schwab in an index target-date fund (about 0.08%) with the stable-value money in a money-market fund would cost about $213. On a $385k plan growing at 6%, the difference compounds to roughly $100,878 over ten years.

The DB annuity has no comparable fee line at all — its cost is the gap between the index and the credited return described in section 2, roughly 2–4 points a year on $737,594 in the years the statement shows. It is not included in the $6,650 figure above because it is a return trade-off rather than a charge, but on the household's balance sheet it is the larger number.

6 · Recommendation

Move the 401(k) and the joint account now; unwind the DB annuity on its own clock

The case for consolidating is not that Schwab's funds will outperform Equitable's — it is that 93% of the household sits in two insurance wrappers sold by one representative, both of which cost more than they show: the 401(k) about 1.7% a year in charges and drag, the DB annuity 2–4 points a year in capped upside. The 401(k) and the joint account can move within a quarter. The DB plan is the bigger prize but has a calendar: the withdrawal charge ends in February 2027, $399,634 of segments mature by the end of 2027, and the rest can be moved as they mature or taken at interim value. Everything else — the Schwab custodial accounts, the 529, the mortgage — is already in the right place and just needs housekeeping.

Method

How the numbers were built

  • All twelve PDFs are scans; each page was read from the image and cross-checked against OCR. Every monthly/quarterly reconciliation on the statements ties (beginning + contributions − fees + earnings = ending), and the 129 DB segment values sum to the printed total of $737,594.
  • The DB "statement" is Equitable's 16 July 2026 Segment Activity Report: it shows segment interim values, the July maturities and the annual-lock history, but no contributions, fees, surrender value or plan funding data. The index-vs-credited comparison uses the 25 annual-lock segment-years it prints; standard segments only show a return at maturity.
  • Statement dates differ: 401(k), joint and 529 are at 30 June 2026; the DB annuity at 16 July; the three Schwab custodial accounts at 31 July 2026. Totals mix those dates.
  • The 401(k) statements provided are pages 1–3 and 8 of 8 (Tommy) and the equivalent for Emma; the missing pages would carry transaction detail and the fee disclosure. The joint account is pages 1–5 of 12; the my529 page 1 of 2.
  • Cost figures: asset charge annualized from Q2; fund expense ratio from T. Rowe Price's 6/30/2026 fact sheet for the R class; SWYFX and SWVXX figures are approximate external inputs; LPL fees from its 1/1/2026 fee schedule; Schwab plan terms from schwab.com. All are listed on the workbook's Assumptions sheet and drive the Cost sheet.
  • Companion workbook: T-V Portfolio Analysis.xlsx (household summary, holdings, cost analysis with ten-year projection, migration plan, assumptions, sources).
  • This is analysis, not tax or legal advice; plan termination, DB-plan decisions and the Roth contribution question should be confirmed with the client's CPA and the plan's TPA/actuary.
