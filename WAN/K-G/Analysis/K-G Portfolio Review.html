1 · Concentration

Two tickers, one employer

The brokerage account is 54% QQQ, 44% CHRW and 2% cash. QQQ is itself concentrated — the Nasdaq-100 puts about half its weight in ten technology companies — but it is at least a hundred stocks. CHRW is one company, and it is the same company that pays the salary and issued the options.

Add the options and the picture sharpens: $924,051 of shares plus $1,241,002 of option value is $2,165,053, or 64% of everything in the folder. Because all 18,246 options are deep in the money they move almost one-for-one with the stock, so the effective position is 24,501 shares — $3,619,533 of notional exposure on a $3,378,502 balance sheet. Every $10 move in CHRW is worth $245,010.

July showed what that means. CHRW fell from $188.34 to $147.73 (−21.6%); the shares lost $254,016 and the options $740,970, while QQQ gave up $80,845 (−6.6%). The combined total fell $1,071,405, or −24.2%, in one month. The dashed line on the chart is the shares alone; the solid line adds the options, whose value goes to zero if CHRW ever trades below the $72.74 and $88.87 strikes.

3 · Tax

The same gain costs half as much after retirement

Three layers apply to a Washington resident selling appreciated stock: federal long-term capital gains (15% up to $545,500 of taxable income for a single filer in 2026, 20% above), the 3.8% net investment income tax above $200,000, and Washington's capital-gains excise — 7% on gains above the annual exemption ($278,000 for 2025; 2026 not yet indexed) and 9.9% on the part above $1 million.

Sold in 2026 on top of a $700k salary, every dollar of gain sits in the 20% federal band, pays NIIT, and the $1,426,168 total crosses WA's $1M tier: about $424,097, or 29.7%. Sold in a retirement year with little other income, a $278,000 slice of gain pays no WA tax at all and about $41,006 federally — 14.8%. Even $400,000 a year comes in at 18.1%. Retirement accounts are exempt from the WA tax entirely, and option-exercise income is ordinary income, not a capital gain, so it never sees the WA tax either.

That gap — roughly $218,056 on today's gains — is the reason to hedge and wait rather than sell now. The unwind takes about six tax years at the exemption pace; the table shows one path, selling CHRW first because it is the single-stock risk and carries the lower gain ratio, then QQQ.

4 · Ways forward

Four routes to Schwab

Option 1

Sell everything now and rebuild at Schwab

For

  • Concentration risk gone immediately.
  • Simplest to execute; cash moves cleanly.

Against

  • About $424,097 of tax in 2026 — 29.7% of the gain — because the sale stacks on $700k of salary and crosses WA's $1M tier.
  • Realises a 21% CHRW drawdown month; nothing recovered.
  • Gives up the far cheaper post-retirement window.
Option 2 · recommended

Hedge now, move in kind, sell in stages after retirement

For

  • Transfer QQQ and CHRW to Schwab in kind — no tax, no fee.
  • Realising ~$278k of gain a year in retirement costs ~15% instead of ~30%: roughly $218,056 saved on the current gains.
  • A collar on QQQ and a plan for the options cap the downside while waiting.
  • Diversified portfolio is built with proceeds as they arrive.

Against

  • Takes about six tax years to unwind fully; market risk persists on the unsold balance.
  • Collars cap upside; option-hedging on CHRW may be barred by company policy.
  • WA exemption and federal brackets can change.
Option 3

Exchange fund / charitable vehicles for part

For

  • An exchange fund (7-year lock-up) diversifies CHRW shares with no sale; a donor-advised fund takes QQQ shares at full value with no gain recognised.
  • Gifting appreciated QQQ instead of cash makes every planned donation ~29% cheaper.

Against

  • Exchange funds take single stocks, rarely ETFs; 7-year illiquidity; fees ~1%+.
  • WA's charitable deduction only counts for Washington-based charities; the federal deduction still works.
  • Only sensible for money not needed for retirement spending.
Option 4

Do nothing until retirement, then sell

For

  • No transaction costs or hedging costs now.
  • Same cheap tax window as Option 2 if markets cooperate.

Against

  • Two-thirds of net worth rides on one employer's share price for 2–3 more years — July alone cost $1,071,405 across shares and options.
  • Options expire Dec 2028 / Feb 2030, and most plans give only 90 days after leaving to exercise — waiting can force everything into one tax year.

6 · Long-term shape

What the portfolio should look like at 55

A single 55-year-old with roughly four to five years before penalty-free retirement-account access, and a decade before Social Security, needs two things the current book does not have: a spending reserve that does not depend on the Nasdaq, and equity exposure broader than two tickers. A reasonable end state is 50–60% diversified equity (total US market, international developed and emerging, a modest value/small tilt), 30–40% high-quality fixed income arranged as a 5-year Treasury/CD ladder so that each year's spending is already in hand, and 5–10% cash. Employer stock at zero; no single company above 5%.

The proceeds arrive in instalments, which suits this: each year's ladder sale funds one more rung of bonds and one more slice of broad equity. A direct-indexing account at Schwab can hold the equity sleeve and harvest losses on the way, and those losses can be used to sell CHRW or QQQ faster than the exemption alone would allow. Between retirement and Social Security the low-income years are also the window for Roth conversions from the company and Solo 401(k)s.
