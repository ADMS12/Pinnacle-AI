2The recommendations

The plan at a glance, and why each piece is there

Structure The trust architecture

  • Revocable living trust as the foundation document, with bypass and QTIP sub-trusts at the first death.
  • Two SLATs and one IDGT sale, drafted as GST-exempt dynasty trusts with Nevada situs, funded with discounted non-voting Institute shares and LLC2 interests.
  • Recapitalization and appraisal first, then gifts; voting shares stay with Ihab.
  • GRAT and CRT funded with shares before any sale of the Institute.
  • Cash reserve before any irrevocable gift. The $200K real estate operating account plus $100K personal already meets the $180K target.

Family design How each share is held

  • Living trust: restate the existing one, don't replace it. Same trust, new document, so existing titles do not have to move twice. Why: the LLC interests and shares may already be titled to it.
  • Children's shares in lifetime trusts, never outright. Why: one child lives in the UK, one is newly divorced, one is 21. Outright distributions would be exposed in each case.
  • Grandchildren's 20% becomes a “grandchildren's trust” open to all grandchildren, present and future. Why: as written, only one branch benefits. Section 3 shows the options.
  • Insurance: present the alternatives first. Why: Ihab does not believe in it. Section 6 shows the four ways to pay the tax and what each costs; the policy is kept as a recommendation because it wins on the numbers.
  • Donor-advised fund in a supporting role. Why: the family already has a foundation it controls. Mission gifts go through the foundation; the DAF handles the rest.
  • SLAT 2's assets chosen property by property. Why: some properties should stay in the estate for the basis step-up; the growth properties should go.

Legacy, business, property Eight further recommendations

  • The foundation as the family's philanthropic and governance hub (section 7).
  • A foreign property plan: local wills in the UK, Egypt and Colombia; the properties stay out of the US trusts; a path to pass the UK properties to the London daughter (section 5).
  • An ESOP feasibility study alongside the market check, because there is no family successor (section 4).
  • A contingency succession agreement naming who runs the Institute the day Ihab cannot, with retention terms for clinical leadership (section 4).
  • A grantor charitable lead trust at the sale that funds the foundation for 20 years from pre-tax dollars and passes the remainder to the children gift-free (section 7).
  • Family governance: a family council, a charter, staggered board seats, and a next-generation grant committee (section 10).
  • Incentive and stewardship provisions in the children's and grandchildren's trusts (section 3).
  • A section 303 redemption reserve inside the Institute as the insurance-free backstop for estate tax (section 6).

Not recommended Two choices, explained

  • The Rockefeller waterfall is optional, not recommended. Why: it depends on permanent policies on each child, and the family's view of insurance makes a multi-generation insurance program unlikely to be maintained. The dynasty trusts do their job without it. It stays available if the children, who will govern the trusts, choose it later.
  • UCLA gifts route through the foundation rather than a DAF. Why: UCLA psychology's autism research is inside the foundation's mission, so the foundation can fund a named research program there. The family keeps the same tax benefit, adds its own name to the work, and gives the children a board seat in the decision.
3Family legacy planning

Four children, four designs, one set of rules

The split is equal. How each share is held is not, because the four children are in four different situations. Every share goes into a lifetime trust rather than outright, and each trust says something different.

The grandchildren's clause needs a decision

The profile says 20% to each child and 20% to “the two grandkids.” Both grandchildren are the 46-year-old daughter's children, so her branch receives 40% of the legacy and the other three children's future children receive nothing. That may be exactly what you intend, since those two grandchildren are the ones you know, but it should be a choice rather than an accident of timing. Three ways to write it:

  • Open class (recommended). 20% to a grandchildren's trust for every grandchild living at the second death, shared equally. The two teenagers are in it today; any later grandchildren join. College funding comes from this trust.
  • Named beneficiaries. 20% to the two named grandchildren only, as the profile reads. Simple, but it has to be revisited at every birth.
  • Per stirpes. No separate grandchildren's share; each child's 25% continues to that child's descendants. Treats every branch alike, but gives the two teenagers less than the profile intends.

Whichever you choose, the trusts are GST-exempt, so none of it is taxed again at the children's deaths.

The rules every trust shares: incentives and stewardship

  • Lifetime dynasty trusts, not staged outright payouts. Each child is a beneficiary for life and can become a co-trustee at 35, with an independent Nevada trustee always in place. That is what protects the shares from divorce, creditors and a second estate tax.
  • Incentive provisions, drafted as guidance to the trustee rather than rigid tests: distributions for education and health without limit; a match of earned income; loans rather than gifts for a home or a business (the family-bank idea from the proposal, without the insurance); support for a child who works in the nonprofit sector or raises children full-time, so the incentive does not punish the choices the family values.
  • Stewardship education as a condition of co-trusteeship: an annual family meeting, a financial literacy course by 25, and a term on the foundation's grant committee by 30.
  • For the 21-year-old: no discretionary distributions beyond education and health until 25, then the full rules; a trusted adult of his choosing may be consulted by the trustee.
  • For the London daughter: the trust holds her share and makes no outright distributions while she is UK resident. US trusts settled by non-UK parents with non-UK assets sit outside UK inheritance tax; money distributed to her does not. Her UK adviser reviews before any distribution, and she should not serve as trustee while resident there.
  • For the divorced daughter: an independent trustee only, no withdrawal rights, and a spendthrift clause, so the share is never hers to be claimed against. Her children's costs can be paid directly to schools and providers.
4Business succession

The Institute: no family successor, three real exits, and one agreement that cannot wait

None of the four children works in the Institute, and Ihab and Vivian own all of it. That settles the succession question: the company will pass to management, to its employees, or to a buyer. The trusts should own the non-voting shares before that happens, so the proceeds, not just the shares, land outside the estate.

Transition risks, as the profile reveals them

  • Key-person risk. A $40M clinical services company whose founder-owner is three years from retirement, with no named successor and no agreement about who runs it the day he cannot.
  • Concentration inside the company. $5M earning 13% is not a bank deposit. If it is private lending or a single position, it is a second business inside the first, and it is at risk in any sale or audit.
  • Expenses through the business. Personal costs paid by the company depress the appraisal defensibly, but they are the first thing a buyer's accountant and an examiner add back.
  • Valuation gap. $40M conservatively against $47M in the plan. The appraisal settles it; the gifts are sized on the appraisal.

Three exits, in the order to study them

  • Management transition with a trustee-held company. Clinical leadership runs it; the trusts and the living trust own it; the family receives distributions. Works if leadership wants to stay and can be retained. Lowest proceeds, highest continuity.
  • ESOP. Sell 30% or more to an employee stock ownership plan. Because the Institute is a C corporation, section 1042 lets Ihab defer the capital gain on the sale, 37% on nearly the whole price, by reinvesting in US operating company securities, and the gain disappears at death through the basis step-up. The company repays the ESOP loan with pre-tax dollars. A $12M first tranche defers about $4.5M of tax.
  • Sale to a strategic or private equity buyer. Autism and behavioral services are an active acquisition market. Highest price, least continuity. The GRAT and CRT go in first.

What to put in place now, whichever exit wins

  • Contingency succession agreement. Names the interim CEO, the board, and the authority of the successor trustee over the voting shares. Signed within 90 days.
  • Retention for clinical leadership. Stay bonuses or phantom equity vesting over three to five years, so the people the value depends on are still there at the transition.
  • Shareholder agreement among Ihab, the SLATs and the IDGT. Right of first refusal, drag-along so the trusts' non-voting shares join any sale, and a formula for redemptions. Required before the first gift.
  • Key-person coverage, corporate-owned. Term coverage on Ihab, paid by the company, as a buyer and lender would expect. Presented because it is a business decision, not a personal one.
  • Clean the books: move personal expenses to salary, document the $5M portfolio's holdings, and restate the last three years before the appraisal.
Should the shares stay in the estate or go into trust? Into trust, for everything except the voting shares. The reason is arithmetic: at the plan's growth the Institute is worth $180M in 2053, and the family's goal is $25M. Every non-voting share that sits in a dynasty trust before the exit sends its share of the proceeds to the children without estate tax; every share still in the estate sends 40% of its proceeds to the IRS first. The voting shares stay with Ihab, inside the restated living trust, until the exit, and the living trust's successor trustee is the person who signs the sale if Ihab cannot.

Individual values, basis and LLC assignments were not in the profile. The appraisal engagement should cover every property so these recommendations can be sized. Egypt and Colombia need local counsel to confirm the succession rules that apply to Ihab's nationality and the property's use.

Asset protection across the portfolio

Two LLCs holding nine properties means one lawsuit at one property reaches the other six in that LLC. Put the highest-value properties in their own LLCs under a holding LLC, keep the cash-flow properties together, and carry umbrella liability coverage of $5M or more. The holding LLC's non-managing units are what SLAT 2 receives, which also raises the discount.

Gift the growth, keep the gain

The rule for choosing what goes into SLAT 2: a property with high growth ahead and modest built-in gain should be gifted, because the growth escapes the estate. A property with a large built-in gain and modest growth should stay, because death erases the gain. Beverly Hills probably stays; Bakersfield and San Bernardino probably go. The appraisal decides.

7The IABA Research & Education Foundation

The foundation as the center of the legacy

Ihab founded the IABA Research & Education Foundation and serves as its chief executive. Its mission, education, training and research for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, is the family's mission: it is what the Institute does, what the three supported charities do, and what the UCLA psychology gift funds. A donor-advised fund would be the default answer for a family without one; this family has something better, with one condition to confirm first.

The condition: public charity or private foundation?

The foundation is a 501(c)(3). The application in the file, a volunteer board, program grants and public funding, reads like a public charity. That status matters more than anything else in this section:

  • If it is a public charity, gifts of appreciated non-voting Institute shares and LLC interests are deductible at full value, up to 30% of income, and the foundation may hold them while the Institute redeems them over time. Everything below assumes this.
  • If it is a private foundation, gifts of closely held stock are deductible only at basis, near zero, it may not own more than 20% of the Institute with the family, and redemptions between the Institute and the foundation are prohibited self-dealing. In that case the appreciated gifts go to the DAF and the foundation receives cash.

Ask Dr. Dotson for the IRS determination letter and the most recent Form 990 Schedule A. This is a one-day question with a large answer.

10Family governance

A structure the four children can share without owning anything together

The children are 25 years apart in age, live on two continents, and are at very different points in life. The governance design keeps them out of each other's money and inside each other's mission.

Family council

Ihab, Vivian and the four children. Meets once a year, in person or online for London. Hears the trustee's report, the foundation's report, and the Institute's status. No votes on anyone's trust; its authority is the charter and the foundation's direction.

Family charter

A short written statement of what the family stands for, drafted with the children in 2027: the disability mission, the expectation of work, the loan-not-gift principle, how the memorial program is run, and how disputes are resolved. Referenced by every trust as guidance to the trustee.

Foundation board

Ihab as chief executive through the transition. Two children join in 2027 on staggered three-year terms, the other two later. A child as chair after Ihab. A grandchildren's grant committee with a $50,000 annual budget from 16. Independent directors with lived experience of disability remain a majority, as the foundation's own application promises.

Trust roles

Independent Nevada trustee for every irrevocable trust. A trust protector, initially an outside adviser, who can replace trustees and fix drafting. Children eligible as co-trustees of their own trusts at 35, after the stewardship conditions. The London daughter does not serve while UK resident. The 42-year-old is the natural first family co-trustee and board member.

What governance does not do. It does not give any child a say over another child's share, it does not require anyone to work in the Institute or the foundation, and it does not make the inheritance conditional on agreement. The money is protected by the trusts; the family is held together by the mission.
