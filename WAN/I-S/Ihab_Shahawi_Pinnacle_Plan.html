Two ways to run the dynasty trust: fill it once, or keep refilling it

The SLATs, the IDGT sale and the ILIT are needed on either path. The choice is what the trust does afterward: hold and distribute what it was given, or run as a family bank that uses life insurance on each generation to refill itself and lends rather than gives.

Path A

Dynasty trust alone: the container

How it works. The trust receives discounted Institute interests once, through the SLATs and the IDGT sale, and holds them outside the estate for as long as the law allows. The ILIT's second-to-die policy pays the estate tax. From then on the trustee invests and distributes at discretion.

What it does well. The simplest path to the stated goal: $25M reaches the children and grandchildren with no estate tax at their deaths, and nothing more to pay or administer beyond the one policy. Money distributed is theirs outright.

Where it falls short. Every distribution shrinks the pool, and money once distributed is exposed to a child's creditors, lawsuits and divorce. Unless returns outpace withdrawals, the trust that was meant to last forever is thin by the third generation.

Path B

Rockefeller “waterfall”: container plus engine

How it works. Same container, same gifts and sale. The difference is what the trust does next: it buys permanent life insurance on the four children, paid from trust income rather than new gifts, and it lends to heirs for homes, businesses and education instead of distributing. Loans come back with interest; each death benefit refills the pool and buys the next generation's policy.

What it does well. The pool never empties, so the trust really can last beyond the grandchildren. Money stays inside, protected from creditors and divorce, and loans teach the next generation to build rather than spend. Each death benefit also gives the trust cash at the moment the family needs it.

Where it costs more. Premiums on each generation for decades, underwriting the children now while they are young and healthy, and a trustee with the discipline to enforce loan terms with family members. It only works if the policies are kept in force.

How to decide

Path A fits if the family prefers simplicity, expects the children to be financially independent, wants money to reach them outright, or if underwriting on the children comes back poorly.

Path B fits if the family wants the trust to last beyond the grandchildren, would rather the children build with loans than receive gifts, has insurable children, and the Institute's distributions can carry the premiums without strain. The $5M a year the company earns suggests they can.

Our read, updated for the family profile. Build Path A now, because it is needed either way. The waterfall depends on permanent policies on each of the four children, and a family that does not believe in life insurance is unlikely to keep a multi-generation insurance program in force, so it is offered as an option the children can switch on later, not as a recommendation. The one decision that cannot wait is underwriting Ihab and Vivian at 63 and 67, so the first policy, which pays the estate tax, is decided on a quote rather than a belief.

4Wealth accumulation

Real estate: make the $20M work harder and travel further

Two LLCs hold nine US properties worth $20M with no debt: Kansas, Missouri, and seven in California including Beverly Hills, Bakersfield, Artesia, San Bernardino and a Pasadena rental that loses money. Seven more properties, five in the UK and one each in Egypt and Colombia, are held personally and not yet valued. None of it is modeled in the plan. That is both a gap in the data and an opportunity: real estate is the family's most flexible asset for liquidity, tax and transfer.

Cost segregation

Reclassify parts of the $17M property into 5, 7 and 15-year lives. With 100% bonus depreciation restored, a large first-year deduction shelters rental income and, if the family qualifies as real estate professionals, other income.

Refinance for liquidity

Borrowing against unleveraged property is tax-free cash. A 50% loan on LLC2 produces $8M of liquidity for reserves, premiums and gifts without selling anything or realizing gain.

1031 and Opportunity Zones

If a property is sold, a 1031 exchange defers the gain; a Delaware Statutory Trust turns active management into passive income for retirement. Opportunity Zone funds, now permanent from 2027, defer and partly exclude gains from any source, including a future sale of the Institute.

Transfer with discounts

LLC interests are ideal gifts: non-managing interests are appraised at 25% to 35% below asset value, so $15M of exemption can move $20M of property, and all future appreciation and rent, to the trusts.

What to confirm first, and what we already know

  • Value, basis, tenants and rents for each of the nine US properties; which sit in LLC1 ($3M, two properties) and which in LLC2 ($17M); whether the Institute is a tenant (related-party leases are a clean way to move cash out of the C corp as deductible rent).
  • Pasadena runs negative: exchange it, gift it to the foundation if appreciated, or sell. Beverly Hills likely has the largest built-in gain and should stay in the estate for the basis step-up; the cash-flow properties are the ones to gift.
  • Values and basis for the UK, Egypt and Colombia properties, and local counsel in each country: UK inheritance tax applies to UK real estate regardless of owner, and Egypt and Colombia apply forced-heirship rules that do not recognize a US trust.
  • Whether either spouse logs enough hours to qualify as a real estate professional.

Why it matters, risks

  • Benefits: liquidity without taxable sales, deductions that offset high-bracket income, discounted transfers that move growth out of the estate, and a retirement income stream that is not the Institute.
  • Risks: leverage adds interest cost and refinancing risk; passive-loss rules limit who can use the depreciation; discounts invite scrutiny without a proper appraisal.
  • Implementation: cost segregation study (8 to 12 weeks), lender term sheets, appraisals timed with the trust funding in section 2.

The decision underneath everything

What happens to the Institute

Every section above depends on one answer: is the Institute sold, sold to its employees, or held under trustee ownership with professional management? No child works in the company today, so a family succession is not on the table unless that changes. Each path has a different tax shape, and the trusts, insurance and retirement plan should be built to match it.

Sell to a strategic or private equity buyer

  • ABA providers have drawn consistent buyer interest; a $5.4M profit stream supports a value in the range the plan uses.
  • A stock sale is taxed once at 20% plus 3.8% plus California (up to 13.3%); an asset sale of a C corp is taxed twice. Negotiate for a stock sale.
  • Pre-sale moves matter most: GRAT or IDGT sale of shares before the letter of intent, charitable gift of shares to a DAF or lead trust, and confirmation of whether any qualified-small-business-stock exclusion is available (health services are generally excluded).

Sell to an ESOP

  • A C corporation selling at least 30% to an employee stock ownership plan lets the owner defer the capital gain by reinvesting in qualified replacement securities.
  • The company deducts contributions used to repay the ESOP loan, and can later elect S status and pay no federal income tax on the ESOP's share.
  • Fits a mission-driven workforce and a founder who wants continuity; requires a trustee, valuation and a long-term financing plan.

Hold it, under trustee ownership and professional management

  • Clinical leadership runs it; the dynasty trusts and the living trust own it; the family receives distributions. Lowest proceeds, highest continuity, and the mission stays with the foundation.
  • Needs what the other two paths need anyway: a contingency succession agreement naming the interim CEO and board, retention terms for the leaders the value depends on, and a shareholder agreement among Ihab and the trusts.
  • Key-person coverage owned by the company is a business decision, not a personal one, and a buyer or lender will expect it whichever path wins.
Our recommendation for the meeting: do not decide today; agree on the question and a 90-day process to answer it, while the steps common to all three paths (living trust, recapitalization, valuation, insurance underwriting, retirement plan census) start now.

Likely objections and responses

  • "I will just sell the company and then plan." After a sale the growth is already in the estate and the discounts are gone; pre-sale trusts and charitable transfers are where most of the tax saving lives. Planning first does not commit him to a sale.
  • "I do not want to give up control." Recapitalize into voting and non-voting shares; he keeps the votes and transfers the value. Trustees can be family or a corporate trustee he chooses.
  • "I don't believe in life insurance." Agree that as an investment it is not one. Reframe it as pre-paying the estate tax at about 40 cents on the dollar, with the IRS's own rules keeping the proceeds out of the estate; the alternatives are a $21M corporate reserve, a 14-year IRS lien, or a bank loan at the worst time. If still no, the 303 reserve is the plan and the company cannot be sold before the second death. Underwriting first, decision later.
  • "A retirement plan means paying for all my employees." True, and the staff cost is the price of a $650K+ corporate deduction. The census model shows the net; a nonqualified plan is the fallback.
  • "The C corp investments are doing fine." They are taxed twice on the way out and invite accumulated earnings tax; the same dollars in a plan, in trusts or in LIHTC credits do more.
  • "Why three firms?" One plan, three licenses: fiduciary advice, insurance placement and business-owner planning each require different registrations. The client sees one team and one cadence.
