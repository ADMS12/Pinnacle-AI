Example 1 · The Shahawi Family Trust: the restated revocable living trust

Purpose and assets

Avoid a California probate worth $3.4M today and $9M in 2053, keep the family's affairs private, and name who votes the Institute's shares if Ihab cannot. The existing trust is twelve years old and is restated, not replaced, so titles already in it stay put. It holds everything the family keeps control of: voting shares, LLC manager interests, the home, Beverly Hills and the cash.

Grantor and trustee

Grantors: Ihab and Vivian, together. Trustees: Ihab and Vivian. This is the one trust where grantor, trustee and beneficiary are the same people, because it is revocable: nothing has left them, so nothing is lost by their controlling it. They must be different people only in the irrevocable trusts that follow.

Beneficiaries and distributions

Ihab and Vivian, for life, with no limits: they may take anything, at any time, for any reason. After the first death the survivor's half stays the same; the deceased spouse's half follows the bypass and QTIP rules (examples 9 and 10). After the second death nothing passes outright: 80% to the four children's lifetime trusts, 20% to the grandchildren's trust.

If a trustee dies

If one spouse dies, the other continues alone as trustee of the survivor's trust and co-trustee of the bypass and QTIP trusts with an independent co-trustee. When neither can serve, the named successor, the 42-year-old son with a corporate co-trustee, takes over by signing an acceptance. No court, no retitling, no change in what the beneficiaries receive.

If a grantor dies

At the first death the trust divides into the survivor's trust (still revocable), the bypass trust and the QTIP trust (both irrevocable). The survivor keeps voting the Institute's shares. At the second death the whole trust becomes irrevocable, the successor trustee pays any tax from the ILIT's proceeds, executes the Institute's succession instructions, and funds the children's and grandchildren's trusts.

What it does not do

It saves no estate tax: revocable assets are counted in full. Its job is probate, privacy, incapacity and instructions. The assets that are meant to leave the estate, the non-voting shares, the growth properties and the policy, go to the irrevocable trusts in the next examples and are never titled here.

How it reaches the heirs. Privately, at the second death, through the children's and grandchildren's trusts, on the schedule the restated document writes today. The London daughter's share is held, not distributed, while she is UK resident; the divorced daughter's share has an independent trustee only.

Example 2 · Two SLATs: Ihab's for Vivian, Vivian's for Ihab

Purpose and assets

Use each spouse's $15M exemption on the assets that will grow most, while the household keeps indirect access. SLAT 1 (2026): non-voting Institute shares, appraised at $15M after a 30% discount. SLAT 2 (2027): non-managing units in LLC2 holding Bakersfield, San Bernardino, Artesia, Kansas and Missouri. California community property is partitioned first so each gift comes from one spouse alone.

Grantor and trustee

Grantor: Ihab for SLAT 1, Vivian for SLAT 2. Trustee: a Nevada trust company for all discretionary decisions, with the beneficiary spouse as co-trustee limited to health, education, maintenance and support. The grantor can never be trustee: any control over the trust he or she funded pulls it back into the estate. Two different trust companies, so the trusts are not mirror images.

Beneficiaries and distributions

The other spouse for life, then the four children and grandchildren. The spouse may receive HEMS distributions from the family co-trustee and anything more from the independent trustee. Children receive at the independent trustee's discretion, as loans for a home or a business before outright money. A “floating spouse” clause names whoever is married to the grantor, in case of divorce.

If a trustee dies

A trust company does not die; if it merges or resigns, the trust protector (an outside adviser named in the document) appoints a replacement. If the beneficiary spouse serving as co-trustee dies, the independent trustee continues alone. Beneficiaries' rights and the trust's tax status do not change.

If a grantor dies

Nothing in the trust changes hands: the assets left the estate at the gift. Two things do change. The trust stops being a grantor trust and files its own return, and the deceased grantor's swap power ends. If instead the beneficiary spouse dies first, the trust continues for the children and the grantor loses the indirect access, which is why neither SLAT holds more than the family can do without.

The estate tax result

About $41M of underlying value leaves the estate using $30M of exemption. At the plan's growth it is worth about $110M in 2053, all outside the 40% tax: roughly $44M of estate tax removed, with GST exemption allocated so the children's shares are not taxed again at their deaths either.

How it reaches the heirs. At the second death each SLAT splits into four equal dynasty trusts, one per child, with an independent trustee, HEMS distributions, loans before gifts, and co-trustee eligibility at 35. Each child's trust continues for that child's own descendants, untaxed.

Example 3 · The IDGT sale: Ihab sells shares to a trust for a note

Purpose and assets

Move a second $21M of Institute value out of the estate without using exemption, after the SLATs have used it. The asset is non-voting shares appraised at $15M, sold to the trust for a nine-year note at the IRS rate. A seed gift of about 10% goes in first so the trust can support the debt. The shares' dividends, from the Institute's distribution policy, make the note payments.

Grantor and trustee

Grantor: Ihab, from his separate property. Trustee: a Nevada trust company. Can Ihab be trustee? No. He keeps a “swap power”, the right to exchange assets of equal value, which makes the trust a grantor trust for income tax without giving him any control that would pull it back into his estate. The trustee signs the note and votes nothing, since the shares are non-voting.

Beneficiaries and distributions

The four children, then their descendants; Vivian is deliberately not a beneficiary, so this trust and SLAT 1 are different. No distributions are expected while the note is outstanding. Afterward the trustee may distribute for HEMS and, in its discretion, beyond it; loans for homes and businesses come before outright money. No age-based payouts: the shares are held for life.

If the trustee dies

A corporate trustee is replaced by the trust protector if it resigns or merges. The note, the shares and the beneficiaries' rights are unchanged; the new trustee simply continues the payment schedule.

If the grantor dies

The shares stay in the trust; only the unpaid balance of the note is in Ihab's estate, at face value, and passes under the living trust. Grantor-trust status ends, so the trust pays its own income tax from then on. Because the tax treatment of a note still outstanding at death is unsettled, the plan sizes the note to be repaid within nine years and well inside Ihab's life expectancy.

Why it pairs with SLAT 1

SLAT 1 spends exemption; the IDGT spends none. Together they move about $43M of underlying Institute value, more than half the company, while Ihab keeps every voting share. Every dollar of income tax Ihab pays on the trust's earnings is one more dollar the trust keeps, with no gift tax.

How it reaches the heirs. At Ihab's death, or earlier if the document says so, the trust divides into four children's dynasty trusts. GST exemption is allocated to the seed gift, which makes the whole trust exempt, so the grandchildren inherit from it untaxed as well.

Example 4 · A two-year GRAT, funded only if a sale of the Institute is coming

Purpose and assets

Capture the jump in the Institute's value at a sale, for the children, with almost no gift. The asset is $10M of non-voting shares placed in the GRAT before any binding agreement, when the appraised value still reflects a private company. If the sale closes at a higher price inside the term, the excess passes to the children's trust free of gift tax.

Grantor and trustee

Grantor: Ihab. Trustee: Ihab, during the term. Same person: yes, because during the annuity term he is the only person entitled to anything, so there is nothing to abuse. At the end of the term the remainder passes to a trust with an independent trustee; Ihab must not control what the children receive.

Beneficiaries and distributions

During the term: only Ihab, who receives two fixed annuity payments, one each year, in cash or in shares. Nobody else may receive anything. After the term: the four children through a remainder trust that follows the same rules as their other trusts (HEMS, loans before gifts, independent trustee). The GRAT is not suited to the grandchildren.

If the trustee dies

Ihab is the trustee, so this is the grantor's death: see the next card. If a successor trustee is needed for any other reason, the document names Vivian, then a corporate trustee, to make the remaining annuity payments and close the term.

If the grantor dies

During the term: the GRAT's assets are included in Ihab's estate as if the GRAT had never existed. Nothing is lost except the legal fees; the shares pass under the living trust. After the term: nothing happens; the remainder already belongs to the children's trust and the annuity payments already came back to Ihab.

When to use it

Only if the 90-day review points to a sale within two to three years. In a hold or an ESOP the GRAT adds little, because the shares' value does not jump. It can be repeated: a “rolling” series of two-year GRATs catches growth in each period with the same near-zero gift.

How it reaches the heirs. The remainder, about $3.5M in the example, lands in the children's remainder trust at the end of year two and is held for them for life under the family's standard trust terms. The annuity Ihab received back can then fund the CLAT or further gifts.

Example 5 · The Grandchildren's Trust: a dynasty trust with GST exemption allocated

Purpose and assets

Deliver the 20% share meant for the grandchildren in a form that is never taxed again and stays open to grandchildren not yet born. Seeded in 2027 with non-managing units of LLC1 (two properties, $3M, appraised at about $2.1M), with GST exemption allocated to the gift; topped up with 20% of the estate at the second death. Every other irrevocable trust in the plan is drafted the same way.

Grantor and trustee

Grantors: Ihab and Vivian, splitting the gift. Trustee: a Nevada trust company, which gives the trust a 365-year life and creditor protection. Grantors as trustees: no. The 42-year-old son serves as distribution adviser, a role that recommends but does not decide, and a trust protector can replace the trustee. A grandchild may become co-trustee of his own share at 35.

Beneficiaries and distributions

The two grandsons, 16 and 14, today, and any grandchild born before the second death. Until 25, tuition and health only, paid straight to the school ($100K a year each from 18). From 25, HEMS, a match of earned income, and loans for a home or business. No outright payout at any age: the share is held for life and passes to each grandchild's own children.

If the trustee dies

The corporate trustee does not die. If the distribution adviser dies, the trust protector appoints another family member, typically the eldest grandchild once over 30. Nothing about the beneficiaries' rights, the GST status or the Nevada situs changes.

If a grantor dies

The 2027 gift is already complete, so nothing moves. At the second death the trust receives its 20% share of the estate and the SLATs' grandchildren provisions, and the trustee begins the college payments if they have not started. Grantor-trust status ends and the trust files its own return from then on.

What GST allocation buys

Without it, a gift to a grandchild's trust pays the 40% GST tax on top of gift or estate tax. With $2.1M of exemption allocated at funding, the whole trust, including the growth and the later 20% top-up (allocated at that time), is exempt for as long as it lasts. Example 12 shows the arithmetic across three generations.

How it reaches the heirs. It never leaves the trust. Each grandchild uses it for life under the rules above, and at each grandchild's death that share continues for his children. The 40% tax that would have applied at every generation does not apply at any of them.

Example 6 · The ILIT: a trust that owns the $20M policy from the first day

Purpose and assets

Create the cash to pay the estate tax at the second death without selling the Institute or the real estate, with money that is never itself taxed. The asset is a new $20M second-to-die policy on Ihab and Vivian, applied for and owned by the trust from the first day, paid with annual gifts of about $350K. Section 6 of the legacy proposal compares this with the three insurance-free alternatives.

Grantor and trustee

Grantors: Ihab and Vivian. Trustee: the 42-year-old son, or a corporate trustee if the family prefers. Can the grantors be trustee? Never. Any “incident of ownership”, the right to change the beneficiary, borrow against the policy or surrender it, puts the full $20M back in the estate. Neither insured may touch the policy.

Beneficiaries and distributions

The four children and the grandchildren, under dynasty terms. During the insureds' lifetimes the only routine distribution is the 30-day Crummey withdrawal right on each gift, which the children are expected not to exercise. The trustee may borrow against or withdraw cash value for a beneficiary's HEMS needs, but never for Ihab or Vivian. After the second death: loans to or purchases from the estate, then lifetime trusts.

If the trustee dies

The successor named in the document, another child or the corporate trustee, takes over; the policy is owned by the trust, not the trustee, so ownership does not change and the carrier is simply notified. Premium gifts and notices continue on the same calendar.

If a grantor dies

First death: a second-to-die policy pays nothing; the survivor continues the gifts, or the trust's own assets pay the premium. Second death: the carrier pays $20M to the trust within weeks, income-tax free and outside both estates. The trustee lends to the estate or buys assets from it so the tax is paid in cash, and holds the rest for the children.

The gift tax mechanics

Six beneficiaries, two donors, $19,000 each: $228,000 a year of premium is covered by annual exclusions, with the balance drawn from exemption on a gift tax return. Each gift triggers a written notice and a 30-day window; the trustee keeps the notices. Skipping a notice is the most common ILIT failure and it is purely administrative.

How it reaches the heirs. After the tax is paid, roughly $13M of proceeds plus whatever the trust bought from the estate sit in the four children's dynasty trusts, untaxed and protected. Examples 11 and 12 cover what the family can and cannot reach from the policy while Ihab and Vivian are alive, and what living-benefit riders do.

Example 7 · The CRT at a sale: 20% of the Institute becomes income and a gift

Purpose and assets

Turn a 37% capital gains tax on a slice of the Institute into lifetime income and a legacy gift to the family's own foundation. The asset is 20% of the non-voting shares, about $12M at a $60M sale, contributed before any letter of intent. The trust sells alongside the family and pays no tax on the gain. A $3.5M property from LLC2 is the alternative asset if the Institute is never sold.

Grantor and trustee

Grantors: Ihab and Vivian. Trustee: Ihab may serve, and usually does, for investing and paying the unitrust amount. Same person: yes, with one condition: an independent special trustee must value the closely held shares at contribution and at each year end, because the grantor may not value his own company for his own deduction.

Beneficiaries and distributions

Ihab and Vivian receive 5% of the trust's value each year, recomputed every January, for as long as either is alive. Nothing else may be distributed to them: no principal, no loans, no extra in a bad year. At the second death the foundation receives everything that is left. Children and grandchildren receive nothing from this trust; their benefit is the tax saved and the other trusts.

If the trustee dies

If Ihab dies, Vivian becomes trustee, then a corporate trustee. The special trustee is replaced by the trust protector. Payments continue on the same schedule; the carrier of the investments is simply changed.

If a grantor dies

Nothing is in either estate: the shares left at contribution, and a spouse's survivor interest in a CRT qualifies for the marital deduction. The 5% payments continue to the survivor unchanged. At the second death the trust ends and the foundation receives the remainder, typically within a few months.

The numbers, together

Capital gains tax avoided about $4.45M; income tax deduction about $4.2M, usable against the sale year's income within the 30% limit; estate tax avoided about $4.8M; $600K a year of income; $12M or more to the foundation. The family gives up the $12M of shares, which is the price of all of the above.

How it reaches the heirs. It does not: this is the family's charitable capital. What reaches the heirs is the $9M of taxes the CRT removed, which the SLATs and the ILIT carry to them, and a funded foundation with their names on its board.

Example 8 · The CLAT at a sale: twenty years of foundation funding, then the children

Purpose and assets

Use the sale year, when income is $40M or more, to fund the foundation for twenty years from pre-tax dollars and pass the remainder to the children with no gift tax. The asset is $10M of cash from the sale proceeds. The annuity is set so that its present value at the IRS rate equals the $10M, which “zeroes out” the gift.

Grantor and trustee

Grantor: Ihab, which makes him taxable on the trust's income during the term in exchange for the upfront deduction. Trustee: an independent trustee. Ihab could serve, but because the charity is the family's own foundation, a trustee who is neither Ihab nor a foundation officer keeps the payments at arm's length and the deduction beyond question.

Beneficiaries and distributions

For twenty years the only distribution is the fixed $800,000 to the foundation, every year, regardless of performance. The children receive nothing until the term ends, then the whole remainder through their lifetime trusts. The foundation's board, with two children on it, decides how the $800,000 is spent.

If the trustee dies

A successor independent trustee is named in the document, with the trust protector as backstop. The annuity continues on schedule; the foundation's payment is a fixed obligation of the trust, not of any person.

If the grantor dies

The trust continues unchanged: the foundation is paid through year 20 and the children receive the remainder. Two consequences: the trust becomes its own taxpayer, and the income tax deduction Ihab took is partly recaptured on his final return for the years not yet paid. Nothing in the trust is in his estate.

Why it fits this family

The foundation gets a budget it can plan around, which lets the children govern a funded institution instead of a fundraising problem. The deduction fits inside the 30% limit at a $40M sale. And the remainder reaches the children at exactly the age the family's rules expect them to take responsibility.

How it reaches the heirs. In year 20, the remainder passes into the four children's dynasty trusts, gift-tax free, under the family's standard terms. The foundation has received $16M by then.

Example 9 · The bypass trust: created inside the living trust at the first death

Purpose and assets

Lock in whatever exemption the first spouse to die still has, so that amount and all its later growth are never taxed in the survivor's estate. Because SLAT 1 used Ihab's 2026 exemption, the bypass trust will hold the indexed increase since then, likely $4M to $6M by the 2040s, funded from his half of the Beverly Hills property and cash. Not a separate document: a formula clause in the restated living trust.

Grantor and trustee

Grantor: the first spouse to die, acting through the living trust's instructions. Trustee: the survivor, with an independent co-trustee. Survivor as trustee: allowed, as long as her own distributions are limited to the HEMS standard; anything beyond that must be decided by the independent co-trustee, or the trust is pulled into her estate.

Beneficiaries and distributions

The survivor for life: all income if she wants it, and principal for health, education, maintenance and support. The children are secondary beneficiaries during her life, at the independent co-trustee's discretion, which lets the trust help a child without touching the survivor's own trust. At her death, the children's trusts in equal shares.

If the trustee dies

The survivor's death is the end of the trust, below. If the independent co-trustee dies or resigns, the trust protector appoints another; the survivor cannot appoint herself to that role.

If the grantor dies

The grantor's death is what creates the trust: the successor trustee divides the deceased spouse's half within nine months, files the estate tax return to report the division, and allocates his remaining GST exemption to the bypass trust. At the survivor's death the trust passes to the children's trusts with no tax, however much it has grown.

Why it still matters after the SLATs

Because exemption is indexed, each spouse accrues new exemption every year after 2026. Without a bypass trust the survivor would need portability to use it, and portability wastes the growth and the GST exemption. The formula captures whatever has accrued, automatically, with no decision needed at a hard time.

How it reaches the heirs. At the survivor's death, directly into the four children's dynasty trusts, untaxed, with GST exemption allocated at the first death so it is untaxed at their deaths too.

Example 10 · The QTIP trust: the rest of the first spouse's half, taxed later

Purpose and assets

Provide for the survivor with no tax at the first death, while the first spouse's document decides who inherits afterward. It receives whatever is left of the deceased spouse's half once the bypass trust is funded: typically his half of the residence, the remaining Beverly Hills interest and the balance of the IDGT note. The voting shares go to the survivor's trust instead, so control stays with the survivor outright.

Grantor and trustee

Grantor: the first spouse to die, through the living trust. Trustee: the survivor, with an independent co-trustee for principal decisions beyond HEMS. Survivor as trustee: allowed. The law requires only that she receive all the income; who manages the assets is the family's choice, and letting her serve keeps the arrangement familiar.

Beneficiaries and distributions

Vivian, and only Vivian, while she lives: all income at least annually, and principal for health, education, maintenance and support. No distributions to children or anyone else during her lifetime, which is the price of the marital deduction. At her death the principal passes to the four children's trusts exactly as Ihab's document directs; she cannot redirect it.

If the trustee dies

Vivian's death ends the trust (below). If the independent co-trustee dies or resigns, the trust protector appoints a successor; Vivian continues as income trustee throughout.

If the grantor dies

That is when the trust begins. The successor trustee funds it within nine months and makes the QTIP election on the estate tax return, which is what defers the tax. At Vivian's death the trust is included in her estate; the tax is paid from the ILIT's proceeds, and the trust passes to the children's trusts.

Why a QTIP rather than outright

An outright inheritance could be left to a new spouse, spent, or claimed by a creditor. The QTIP gives Vivian everything she can use and nothing she can give away. It also lets the executor make a partial election, choosing how much tax to pay at the first death if rates or exemptions have changed by then.

How it reaches the heirs. At the survivor's death, after the tax is paid from the ILIT, the principal passes into the four children's dynasty trusts under the first spouse's instructions.

Appendix · HEMS: Health, Education, Maintenance and Support

HEMS is the distribution standard used throughout this plan wherever a family member serves as trustee. It is the “ascertainable standard” recognized by the Internal Revenue Code (sections 2041 and 2514): a trustee limited to it may distribute income or principal for a beneficiary's health, education, maintenance and support, and nothing else, without the trust being treated as the beneficiary's own property.

What each component covers

HealthMedical and dental treatment, health insurance premiums, mental health therapy, vision care, wellness-related elective procedures, long-term care, and specialized medical equipment.
EducationTuition for primary and secondary school, trade programs and higher education (undergraduate, graduate and professional degrees), study abroad, textbooks and supplies, and living expenses while enrolled. In this plan, tuition is paid directly to the school.
Maintenance and supportThe ordinary living expenses needed to keep the beneficiary's accustomed standard of living: housing (rent, mortgage payments, property taxes), utilities, groceries, transportation and basic lifestyle costs. “Accustomed” is measured at the time the trust is funded, which is why the standard protects without pampering.

Not covered by HEMS: gifts to others, luxury purchases beyond the accustomed standard, business capital, and paying a beneficiary's debts. In this plan those come, if at all, from the independent trustee as loans or discretionary distributions.

Why the standard matters in this plan

  1. Estate tax protection. A beneficiary who is also a trustee, or a family member serving as trustee, would have the trust counted in his or her own estate if distributions were unrestricted. Limiting that trustee to HEMS is what lets Vivian serve as co-trustee of SLAT 1, Ihab of SLAT 2, the survivor of the bypass and QTIP trusts, and each child of his or her own trust at 35, without pulling the trust back into an estate.
  2. Asset and creditor protection. Because distributions are tied to an ascertainable standard rather than available on demand, a creditor, a plaintiff or a divorcing spouse cannot compel the trustee to pay out trust funds to satisfy a claim. This is the protection the divorced daughter's and the London daughter's trusts rely on.
  3. Guidance for the trustee. It gives the trustee a legal yardstick for every request: meaningful support for the beneficiary, no premature depletion of the trust, and a clear basis for declining unreasonable requests.
How it is used here. Family trustees are limited to HEMS; the independent Nevada trustee holds any broader discretion, including loans for homes and businesses and the match of earned income. The two work together: the family member can meet everyday needs, and the professional trustee makes the decisions that must stay independent.

Appendix · CLAT explained: the charitable lead annuity trust

What it is, and how it works

A charitable lead annuity trust is an irrevocable trust that pays a fixed amount every year to a charity for a set number of years, and then hands whatever is left to the family, usually to trusts for the children. The charity's interest comes first (it “leads”); the family's interest is the remainder. It is the mirror image of the charitable remainder trust, where the family is paid first and the charity receives what is left.

The arithmetic. The annuity is sized so that its value, discounted at the IRS rate published each month (about 5% in 2026), equals what went in. The IRS then treats the family's remainder as worth nothing, so the gift is “zeroed out.” If the trust earns more than that rate, the excess reaches the children free of gift and estate tax. If it earns less, the charity is still paid in full and the children receive less or nothing.

Typical assets. Cash after a sale; marketable securities; income-producing real estate or non-managing LLC units, which add a valuation discount; dividend-paying non-voting shares. The asset has to produce the cash for the annuity, or the trustee must sell part of it each year. A residence or an asset with no cash flow is a poor fit.

Tax treatment

Income taxGrantor CLAT: the donor takes an upfront deduction equal to the value of all the charity's payments, and then pays income tax on the trust's earnings each year; no further deduction for the annual payments. The deduction is capped at 30% of income (20% for appreciated property) with a five-year carryforward, and is partly recaptured if the donor dies during the term. Non-grantor CLAT: no upfront deduction; the trust pays its own tax and deducts what it gives to charity without limit.
Gift taxThe taxable gift is the value of the remainder on the day of funding. A “zeroed-out” CLAT makes that close to $0, so no exemption is used however large the trust.
Estate taxThe assets and all their growth are out of the donor's estate. A CLAT can also be created at death (a testamentary CLAT) inside the living trust, which earns an estate tax charitable deduction for the value of the charity's payments. The remainder should go to children, not grandchildren: GST exemption cannot be allocated efficiently until the term ends.

Advantages

  • Moves growth to the children with no gift tax and no exemption
  • Funds the charity now, from pre-tax dollars in a high-income year
  • Principal returns to the family at the end
  • Term and payment can be tuned to the asset's cash flow; best when the IRS rate is low

Disadvantages

  • The charity is paid in full even in bad years; the family bears the risk
  • Irrevocable; the donor pays tax on income he does not receive (grantor CLAT)
  • Deduction recapture if the donor dies during the term; annual Form 5227 and administration
  • Inefficient for grandchildren; needs an independent trustee when the charity is the family's own foundation

Appendix · Section 303: the Institute buys back shares to pay the estate tax

What it is, and why it exists

The estate tax is due in cash nine months after death, but a private company's value is not cash. A company buying back shares is normally taxed to the shareholder as a dividend. Section 303 makes an exception for an estate: a redemption of enough stock to pay the estate tax and the costs of death is treated as a sale, and because the shares took a new basis at death, the sale produces no income tax. The company hands the estate the cash without the family selling to outsiders.
