What a TRUST is, and the four choices that shape one

Revocable or irrevocable

Revocable: you can change or cancel it, so nothing has really left you. It helps with probate and incapacity, never with estate tax. Irrevocable: you give up the right to take it back, and in exchange the assets and all their future growth leave your estate.

Who pays its income tax

A grantor trust is ignored for income tax: the person who created it keeps paying tax on its income, even though the assets are out of the estate. Every tax dollar paid is a dollar the trust keeps, with no gift tax. A non-grantor trust files its own return at compressed brackets.

Inside or outside the estate

Federal estate tax is 40% on everything above the exemption, $15 million per person in 2026. Assets in a revocable trust count. Assets given to an irrevocable trust, plus everything they earn afterward, do not. The goal is to move future growth out while the gift is still small.

Where it lives, how long it lasts

A trust can be governed by another state's law. Nevada and South Dakota allow a trust to run for centuries or forever, charge no state income tax on what it keeps, and shield assets from a beneficiary's creditors and divorces. California trusts face state tax and a time limit.

Three ways value leaves an estate, and all three appear in the plan. Gifts within the exemption. Discounts, because a non-voting minority share of a private company is appraised at 25% to 35% less than its pro-rata value. And growth above the IRS rate, which a sale to a grantor trust or a GRAT hands to the heirs without using exemption at all.

The six trusts in the plan, one by one

Revocable living trust

Control now, no court later

You are grantor, trustee and beneficiary all at once, and you can rewrite or cancel it any time. At death a successor trustee distributes privately, with no probate; if you become incapacitated, they step in without a conservatorship. It does nothing for estate tax, and it only works for assets actually retitled into it.

SLAT, spousal lifetime access trust

Out of the estate, not out of reach

One spouse makes an irrevocable gift to a trust for the other spouse and the descendants. The gift uses exemption; the assets and their growth are out of both estates, yet the beneficiary spouse can still receive distributions, so the household keeps indirect access. Two SLATs must be deliberately different or the IRS treats them as cancelling out.

IDGT, intentionally defective grantor trust

Sell, don't give, and keep paying the tax

An irrevocable trust that is out of your estate but “defective” for income tax, so you still pay tax on what it earns, a gift the IRS does not count. Instead of giving assets, you sell them to the trust for a promissory note: no capital gain is triggered, and everything the assets earn above the note's modest interest rate stays with the heirs.

GRAT, grantor retained annuity trust

A short bet on a jump in value

You place an asset in trust for a fixed term, often two to five years, and take back annual payments that return the full value plus the IRS's published rate. Whatever the asset earns above that rate passes to the heirs with almost no gift. If you die during the term the asset comes back into the estate, which is why terms are kept short.

Dynasty, or GST, trust

Taxed once, then never again

Estate tax normally hits again at each generation. A dynasty trust is built to last for generations and has generation-skipping exemption allocated to it, so nothing is taxed at the children's deaths, or the grandchildren's. With a Nevada or South Dakota situs it can run indefinitely. Any irrevocable trust in the plan can be drafted this way.

ILIT, irrevocable life insurance trust

Cash on the day it is needed

The trust, not the family, owns the policy, so the death benefit is not counted in the estate. Premiums are gifted each year, and the beneficiaries' brief right to withdraw them makes the gifts qualify for the annual exclusion. At death the trust receives the proceeds tax-free and can lend to, or buy assets from, the estate so the tax is paid without a forced sale.

Four more vehicles worth knowing

Charitable remainder trust

Income for you, remainder to charity

You give an appreciated asset to the trust, which sells it with no capital gains tax and pays you, or you and your spouse, an income for life or a term of years. What is left goes to charity, and you get an immediate deduction for the value of that remainder. Best when selling a building or company stock and wanting income rather than a lump sum.

Charitable lead trust

Charity first, family after

The mirror image. Charity receives payments for a set term, and whatever remains passes to the family. The taxable gift to the family is reduced by the value of the charity's payments, so an asset that grows faster than the IRS rate can pass with little or no exemption used. A natural way to fund a multi-year pledge and move wealth at the same time. Explained in full in the appendix of the Shahawi examples deck.

Bypass (credit shelter) trust

First exemption used, growth kept out

At the first spouse's death, assets up to that spouse's exemption go into a trust for the survivor instead of outright. The survivor can draw income and, within limits, principal, but the assets and all their later growth stay outside the survivor's estate. Portability can carry an unused exemption over instead, but it does not protect growth or carry GST exemption.

QTIP marital trust

Provide for the spouse, decide the heirs

QTIP stands for Qualified Terminable Interest Property. Assets above the bypass amount go to a marital trust that qualifies for the unlimited marital deduction, so no tax is due at the first death. The surviving spouse must receive all the income, but the first spouse's document fixes who inherits afterward. Common in blended families or wherever the couple wants the plan locked in. These assets are taxed in the survivor's estate.

Where these could enter the Shahawi plan. The bypass and QTIP trusts are not separate documents: they are instructions inside the living trust that take effect at the first death. A charitable remainder trust becomes relevant if the Institute or a building is sold and the family wants income from the proceeds. A charitable lead trust is one way to fund the UCLA commitment while moving growth to the children.

A fifth vehicle: the Rockefeller “waterfall”, or family bank

A dynasty trust can only be filled once by gift. The waterfall gives it a refill: the trust owns permanent life insurance on each generation, every death benefit pours in free of income and estate tax, and the trust lends to heirs rather than handing money out. Loans come back with interest, the trust buys the next generation's policy, and the pool never runs dry.

Why this list. The estate is headed to $181 million against about $30 million of combined exemption, so the job is to move future growth out, not to shelter what exists. Each tool does one thing: the living trust handles probate and incapacity; the SLATs and IDGT sale spend exemption on discounted Institute stock while the family keeps access; the GRAT matters only if a sale is coming; GST allocation makes the transfer last two generations instead of one; and the ILIT pays the tax that remains so nothing is sold under pressure. None of it gives up control of the Institute, and none of it is irrevocable before the appraisal.

In what order

  1. 1Living trust signed, and the LLC and Institute interests retitled into it. The Institute recapitalized into voting and non-voting shares.
  2. 2Appraisal of the Institute and the real estate, with discounts, and insurance underwriting, both started now.
  3. 3SLATs funded and the IDGT sale closed on the appraised values, GST exemption allocated, Nevada or South Dakota situs chosen.
  4. 4ILIT buys the survivorship policy. The waterfall begins.
  5. 5GRAT only if a sale of the Institute becomes likely.
