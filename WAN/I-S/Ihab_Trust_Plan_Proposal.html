2The vocabulary, once

What a trust is, and the four choices that make them different

Every trust in this plan has these same three parties. What changes from one to the next is the rulebook.

Revocable or irrevocable

Revocable: you can change or cancel it, so nothing has really left you. It fixes probate and incapacity, never estate tax. Irrevocable: you give up the right to take it back, and in exchange the assets and all their future growth leave your estate.

Who pays its income tax

A grantor trust is ignored for income tax: Ihab keeps paying the tax on what it earns even though the assets are out of his estate. Every tax dollar he pays is a dollar the trust keeps, and it is not a gift. A non-grantor trust files its own return at compressed brackets.

Inside or outside the estate

Federal estate tax is 40% on everything above the exemption, $15 million per person in 2026, so $30 million for the two of you. Assets in a revocable trust count. Assets given to an irrevocable trust, plus everything they earn afterward, do not.

Where it lives, how long it lasts

A trust can be governed by another state's law. Nevada and South Dakota allow a trust to run for centuries or forever, charge no state income tax on what it keeps, and shield assets from a beneficiary's creditors and divorces. California trusts face state tax and a time limit.

Three ways value leaves an estate, and the plan uses all three. Gifts within the $30M exemption. Discounts, because a non-voting minority share of a private company is appraised at 25% to 35% less than its pro-rata value, so $15M of exemption can move about $21M of real value. And growth above the IRS rate, which a sale to a grantor trust or a GRAT hands to the heirs without using any exemption at all.
3Trust strategy 1 of 7Now

Revocable living trust: control now, no court later

This is the foundation document. It does nothing for estate tax, but without it the Institute, both LLCs and the home go through a California probate that is public, takes a year or more, and would cost about $3.4 million today and $9.0 million in 2053 on RightCapital's assumptions.

Purpose, and what goes into it

  • Purpose. Avoid probate, keep the family's affairs private, and name who runs things if either of you is incapacitated, without a court-appointed conservator voting the Institute's shares.
  • Assets to retitle. The voting shares of the Institute, the LLC1 and LLC2 membership interests, the residence, and the joint cash account. A trust only controls what it owns; an unfunded trust is the most common estate-planning failure we see.
  • Assets that stay out. Non-voting shares headed for the SLATs and the IDGT sale, and the new life insurance, which the ILIT must own from day one.
  • During your lifetimes. Nothing changes. You are the trustees and the beneficiaries, you file the same tax returns, and you can amend or revoke it at any time.

What the trust does at each death

At the first death

The survivor keeps control of the Institute's voting shares through the survivor's trust. The first spouse's $15M exemption is locked into the bypass trust so that its growth is never taxed again, something portability alone does not do. Income from the QTIP must go to the survivor each year; its principal is taxed at the second death.

At the second death

The successor trustee, whom you name now, distributes to the four children in trust rather than outright, pays any charitable bequest to UCLA, and executes the business succession instructions: who votes the shares, whether the Institute is sold, and on what terms. The $20M and $5M legacy goals are written here.

Applied to Ihab and Vivian

Retitle the LLC interests and voting shares this quarter, before the recapitalization and appraisal. Name a successor trustee who can run or sell a $47M company, which may mean a corporate trustee alongside a child. Pair it with pour-over wills, powers of attorney and health care directives.

4Trust strategy 2 of 7Now

Irrevocable trusts: where the growth goes to live

An irrevocable trust is the only structure that takes future growth out of the estate. For this family the growth is enormous: the Institute's retained profits are projected to add $113 million by 2053. The plan uses one irrevocable trust for each job, and most of them are drafted as grantor trusts so Ihab keeps paying their income tax, which is itself a tax-free transfer.

Purpose and protection

  • Estate tax. Whatever is given to the trust, and everything it earns afterward, is outside the 40% tax. A $21M gift of discounted shares today is worth about $56M in 2053 on the plan's growth rate; all $56M escapes the estate, not just the $21M.
  • Asset protection. With a Nevada or South Dakota trustee, the assets are beyond the reach of a beneficiary's creditors, lawsuits and divorces, and beyond Ihab's own creditors once the gift is complete and seasoned.
  • Appropriate assets. Things expected to grow faster than the IRS rate: non-voting Institute shares first, LLC2 real estate second. Not the home, not the cash reserve, not anything the family needs back.

The IDGT sale: moving value without using exemption

The note stays in Ihab's estate at face value, so the sale freezes $15M and removes the growth. A “seed” gift of about 10% of the sale price goes in first so the trust can support the debt.

Applied to the Shahawis. Two irrevocable trusts carry the plan: a SLAT for each spouse (next section) funded by gift, and one IDGT that buys shares for a note. All three are drafted as dynasty trusts with GST exemption allocated (section 9), sited in Nevada, and with a trust protector who can fix drafting problems later without a court. Combined, about $64M of today's underlying value moves out of a $67M estate, while Ihab keeps every voting share.
5Trust strategy 3 of 7Now

SLAT: out of the estate, not out of reach

The spousal lifetime access trust is the answer to the question every business owner asks about giving: “what if we need it back?” Each spouse funds a trust for the other spouse and the descendants. The gift uses exemption and leaves the estate, but the household keeps indirect access through the beneficiary spouse.

How it works

Which assets, and why they differ

  • SLAT 1 (Ihab funds, 2026): non-voting Institute shares appraised at $15M after a 30% discount, about $21.4M of underlying value. The highest-growth asset goes first.
  • SLAT 2 (Vivian funds, 2027): LLC2 real estate interests appraised at $15M after discount, about $20M of underlying value. A different asset, a different year, a different trustee and different distribution terms.
  • Why they must differ. If the two trusts mirror each other, the IRS “uncrosses” them under the reciprocal trust doctrine and puts both back in the estate. Timing, assets, trustees and terms all have to be visibly different.
  • Lifetime access. Vivian can receive distributions from SLAT 1 for health, education, maintenance and support, and vice versa. The household's lifestyle does not depend on it: salaries of $700K now and $660K of distributions later cover the $360K of spending.

If one spouse dies

The SLAT that spouse funded continues for the survivor and the children: access is unchanged. The SLAT the survivor funded loses its beneficiary spouse, so the survivor cannot reach it. That is why neither SLAT should hold more than the family can afford to lose access to, and why the ILIT provides separate liquidity.

If the marriage ends

The former spouse remains a beneficiary unless the trust says otherwise. Both SLATs are drafted with a “floating spouse” clause, so the beneficiary is whoever is married to the grantor at the time, and the trustee holds discretion over all distributions. This is a drafting detail that must be in the document from day one.

The estate tax saving

Two SLATs move about $41M of underlying value out of the estate using $30M of exemption. At the plan's growth rate that value is roughly $110M in 2053. At 40%, the SLATs alone remove about $44 million of estate tax, and nothing taxable is left behind except the growth the family chose to keep.

6Trust strategy 4 of 7Now

ILIT: cash on the day it is needed, outside the estate

Life insurance you own is part of your taxable estate: a $20M policy in Ihab's name pays $12M after estate tax. The same policy owned by an irrevocable life insurance trust pays $20M, income-tax free and estate-tax free, and the trust can lend to the estate or buy assets from it so the tax is paid without selling the Institute. The family has $50,000 of coverage today and a $14.8M tax bill if both passed tomorrow.

The three-year rule means a policy transferred from Ihab to the trust stays in the estate for three years, so the new policy is applied for by the trust itself.

Charitable remainder trust: sell without the gain, keep an income, and UCLA gets what is left

You give an appreciated asset to the trust. The trust sells it and pays no capital gains tax, pays you and Vivian an income for life, and gives whatever remains to UCLA at the second death. You get an income tax deduction now for the value of that remainder, and the asset is out of the estate. The catch is that UCLA waits, and the deduction is capped at 30% of income each year.

8Trust strategy 6 of 7Now

Donor-advised fund: the simpler way to give to UCLA every year

A donor-advised fund is a charitable account you control. You put assets in, take the deduction that year, and recommend grants to UCLA on whatever schedule you like. It is not a trust, has no lawyer, no tax return and no payout rules, and it accepts the same appreciated assets a CRT does. For annual giving it beats the CRT on every count except one: it pays you no income.

Recommendation: a DAF now, a CRT at the sale

Fund the DAF with appreciated assets, not cash. Giving $175,000 of discounted non-voting shares or LLC2 interests each year instead of cash produces the same deduction and also avoids the capital gains tax the family would otherwise pay on that value, about $65,000 a year at 37.1% on near-zero basis.

Pre-fund the pledge. Four years of gifts, $700,000, fit within the 30% of income limit ($215,000 a year, with a five-year carryforward), so the whole UCLA commitment can be inside the DAF by 2029, before retirement lowers the family's income and the deduction's value with it.

Let the Institute redeem the shares. Once the DAF holds non-voting shares, the Institute can buy them back for cash, at a later date and without a prior agreement. UCLA is paid in cash, the family's ownership is unchanged, and no gain was recognized by anyone.

Keep the CRT for a liquidity event (section 7, example B), where it does what a DAF cannot: turn a 37% tax into a lifetime income.

9Trust strategy 7 of 7Now

Dynasty trust: taxed once, then never again

Estate tax is not a one-time event. Without planning, what the children inherit is taxed again when they die, and again at the grandchildren's deaths. A dynasty trust is drafted to last for generations and has generation-skipping transfer (GST) exemption allocated to it, $15 million per spouse, so nothing inside it is taxed at the children's deaths, or the grandchildren's. Every irrevocable trust in this plan is drafted this way.

A generational example: the $41M the SLATs hold

What the dynasty drafting adds

  • Multi-generation transfer. The trust names the children as beneficiaries, then the grandchildren, then their descendants. Each generation can use the assets; none of them owns them, so none of them is taxed on them.
  • Asset protection. Sited in Nevada or South Dakota with an independent trustee, the assets are not reachable by a child's creditors, a lawsuit, or a divorcing spouse. A child who runs the Institute can do so without putting the family's capital at risk.
  • Estate tax minimization. GST exemption is allocated when the trust is funded, at the discounted value. Thirty million of exemption protects $41M of value today and everything it grows into, forever.
  • The waterfall option. With permanent life insurance on each generation and a policy of lending rather than distributing, the same trust refills itself at every death. The ILIT starts it; the children's policies continue it.
  • Applied here. SLAT 1, SLAT 2, the IDGT and the ILIT are all drafted as dynasty trusts. The $20M and $5M legacy goals are met through them rather than by outright bequests.

11Distribution modeling

Who controls what, and who receives what, at each stage

While both spouses are alive

  • Control. You vote every share of the Institute through the living trust. The SLATs and IDGT hold only non-voting shares, and an independent trustee administers them.
  • Income. Unchanged: salaries, then distributions from 2036 (the main plan brings that forward to 2029). Ihab pays the trusts' income tax, which is the point.
  • Distributions. The SLATs can distribute to the other spouse; the DAF grants to UCLA; the children receive nothing yet unless you choose to.

After the first spouse passes

  • Administration. The living trust divides into three sub-trusts within nine months; the survivor is trustee of all three with an independent co-trustee for the bypass and QTIP.
  • Income continuation. All QTIP income goes to the survivor by law; the bypass trust and the deceased spouse's SLAT distribute at the trustee's discretion. Social Security survivor benefit replaces the smaller check.
  • Ownership changes. Nothing is sold. The survivor's half stays revocable; the first spouse's half is now irrevocable and outside the survivor's estate.

After the second spouse passes

  • Children. The $20M legacy is met from the dynasty trusts, in continuing trusts for each child rather than outright, protected from creditors and divorce and untaxed at their deaths.
  • Charity. UCLA receives the CRT remainder and any charitable bequest; the children take over the DAF as advisors so the family's giving continues.
  • The Institute. The succession instructions decide whether a child-manager takes the voting shares or the trustee sells to a buyer or to the employees. Either way the estate tax is paid from the ILIT, so no one is forced to sell under a deadline.
