1 · The rule

Your equity must stay above a minimum share of the holdings

When you borrow to buy stock, the broker sets a maintenance requirement: your equity must remain at least a set percentage of the value of the securities. The regulatory floor is 25%; Robinhood's house level on a portfolio like this is typically 30%, and it can set a higher figure on individual volatile stocks.

The catch is that the loan does not get smaller when the market falls — only your equity does. Every dollar the holdings lose comes out of your $220,554, not Robinhood's $216,910. So as prices drop, equity shrinks twice as fast in percentage terms as the holdings do, and the ratio of equity to holdings slides toward the minimum.

Today equity is 50% of holdings, so there is no call. The chart works out where the line is crossed: if the holdings fall 29%, they are worth about $309,872, the loan is still $216,910, and equity is down to about $92,962 — exactly 30%. Any further fall triggers a margin call: Robinhood requires you to deposit cash or sell holdings, usually within days, and if you don't it sells positions of its choosing for you. With a 25% minimum the same arithmetic gives room for a 34% fall.

Put another way: with two dollars of stock for every dollar of your own money, a 10% market move is a 20% move in your equity, and a 29% move wipes out nearly 60% of it. That is the price of the extra upside leverage provides — and the reason the cushion matters more than the return.

3 · Why this matters now

A 29% fall is not a remote scenario

  • The holdings are volatile. The account is concentrated in large technology stocks and a long tail of quantum-computing, uranium, space and silver positions. The sister SEP IRA, which holds many of the same names, fell 13% in July 2026 alone and swung by more than 13% in three separate months this year. Declines of 30–50% in this kind of portfolio have happened several times in the last decade.
  • Forced sales happen at the worst moment. A margin call arrives after the fall, so the positions are sold near the low, turning a temporary drop into a permanent loss. Robinhood chooses what to sell.
  • A transfer freezes the account. Moving the account to Schwab takes roughly five to seven business days, during which nothing can be traded. If a call happens in that window, Robinhood liquidates and you cannot respond. Schwab may also apply stricter requirements on arrival, moving the trigger closer.
  • The loan is expensive to keep. Interest is about $732 a month today (about 4.5% a year) and would roughly double at Schwab's posted rate — around $17,000 a year against about $2,700 of dividends.

The remedy is simple: reduce or clear the loan before the transfer. Paying it off entirely ($216,910) makes a margin call impossible; paying it down to about $122,000 means the holdings would have to fall 60% before a call.
