The $125,100 loan to the owner

Over several years the owner took $125,100 out of the company as a "loan": no written note, no interest, no repayments, no collateral. It is the largest asset on the books, and without it the company is about $125K underwater.

If the IRS calls it what it looks like, a distribution or hidden wages, the bill is roughly $28,000 to $35,000 plus penalties. Lenders and the IRS both read this line first. Every scenario that follows starts by fixing it.

The yardstick: the household spends about $17,000 a month, $204,000 a year. Once the spouse is on B LLC's payroll her $1,082 a month of vehicle cost becomes a deductible business expense, so while she is an employee the household is checked against $16,000 a month, $192,000 a year.

Four gaps

Undocumented loan
$125,100 and growing each year
No health insurance
unlimited medical risk, no deduction
Minimal life protection
one $165K policy ($15K cash value) against $171K of guaranteed debt and $204K a year of living costs

nothing set aside at age 31

  • Stop all new withdrawals from B LLC. Sign a note at the 4% federal rate and pay $25,000 of principal a year.

  • This turns a tax exposure into an ordinary debt and sends cash back to the company to retire its 21% loans.

2

Bring the spouse into the business

Make it a family business

  • The spouse is paid $12,000 a year in W-2 wages for documented part-time work (bookkeeping, scheduling, marketing).
  • Her wages are deductible to B LLC and taxed on the joint return, so income tax is a wash; the net cost is about $2K of payroll tax.
  • As a B LLC employee her vehicle ($1,082 a month, $12,984 a year) becomes a business expense on B LLC's books, deductible to the extent of documented business use. Household living costs drop from $17K to $16K a month ($192,000 a year) while she is on payroll.
  • What it buys: Social Security credits for her, a documented role, and eligibility for the retirement plan in the next step.

3

Fund retirement and health, set aside for growth, clear the loan in two years

Put the business to work for the family

  • Owner salary $140,000; spouse $12,000. Both join a solo 401(k): $24,500 each from salary plus 25% from the company, $89,500 a year including Roth IRAs.
  • B LLC pays the $18,000 family health premium, fully deductible.
  • B LLC sets aside $100,000 of after-tax cash each year for expansion.
  • Living costs: with the spouse on B LLC payroll her $1,082 a month vehicle cost is a B LLC expense ($12,984 a year, reducing the K-1), so the household bar is $16K a month ($192,000 a year) while she is an employee. The shortfall tiles use that bar.
  • The $125,100 loan is repaid $62,550 in 2026 and $62,550 in 2027 with distributions the owner immediately pays back, tax-free because the income is already taxed.

4

Protect the family with cash-value life insurance

Insure the people who make it work

  • The owner already holds a $165K policy with $15K of cash value. It stays in force, but it covers less than one year of living costs; the plan adds permanent policies of $24,000 a year for the owner (about $1.5M) and $12,000 for the spouse (about $1M), paid from personal, after-tax money.
  • If the owner dies, $1.5M income-tax free replaces about ten years of spendable income and clears the company debt he has guaranteed.
  • Cash value grows tax deferred and can be borrowed against later, a second retirement reserve.
  • A $1.5M 20-year term policy costs under $1,000 a year and can be bound now while the permanent policies are designed.

5

A sibling C corporation pays for protection and the payoff

Let a second company carry the load

  • The owner forms a C corporation alongside B LLC (it cannot own B LLC without ending the S election). It handles sales, marketing, HR and management and bills B LLC $320,000 in 2026 and $440,000 in 2027.
  • The C corp runs payroll, the 401(k) and the health plan (a tax-free benefit there), and pays its own tax at about 28%.
  • What it keeps is lent to the family on documented 4% notes: the $36,000 of premiums first (policies pledged as collateral), then the loan payoff to B LLC, then money for acquisitions.
  • At death the policies repay the C corp and the family keeps the rest tax free.

Questions, decisions and next steps

Decisions to make today

  • Pace of the loan payoff: two years (steps 3 to 5) or five years (steps 1 and 2)?
  • Owner salary: $140,000 as modeled, or a different level?
  • Expansion set-aside: $100K a year starting 2026, or start in 2027? At $16K a month of living costs, 2026 only works if the set-aside or the payoff pace gives way.
  • Life insurance: permanent policies now, or term now and permanent later?
  • Form the C corporation for 2027, or stay with one entity?

Action items in the next 30 days

  • Sign a 4% note on the $125,100 loan and make the first payment.
  • Put the owner and spouse on payroll; set withholding to cover the projected tax.
  • Enroll the family health plan through B LLC.
  • Keep the existing $165K policy in force and review its cost against the $15K cash value; bind a $1.5M term policy on the owner; request permanent-policy illustrations for both.
  • Open the solo 401(k) and Roth IRAs.
  • Send every loan repayment to the Fundbox and Headway balances.

For the next 90 days

  • Preparer to confirm the credit carryforwards and rerun 2026 estimates.
  • Attorney to draft the services agreement and split-dollar notes if the C corp proceeds.
  • Decide the acquisition plan that justifies retained earnings.
  • Review travel, meals and vehicle spending ($90K in 2025).
  • Revisit the California pass-through entity election.
Recommended path: execute steps 1 to 3 now, bridge protection with term insurance, design the permanent policies, and form the C corporation for 2027 if the owner wants to retain earnings for acquisitions and fund the policies through it.
