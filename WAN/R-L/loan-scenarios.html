The federal savings look small only because 2025 federal tax was already zero. The real federal effect was preserving credit carryforward: $10,543 of solar credit survived into 2026 instead of being spent, and at 2026 rates that carryforward is worth its face value.

Two indirect effects

  • The interest on borrowed money was fully deducted even though part of the borrowing funded the owner's loan. See section 3; up to about $12K of the $30.9K is exposed.
  • No imputed interest was reported. A below-market loan over $10,000 between a corporation and its shareholder is governed by §7872. The company should have reported interest income at the applicable federal rate, about 4% of the average balance, or roughly $4,300 for 2025, which would have passed through to the owner's K-1. It was not.
The cumulative picture: $125,100 of cash has reached the owner over several years with no income tax, no payroll tax and no interest, while the company borrowed at merchant-cash-advance rates and deducted the cost. That is the pattern the IRS calls a constructive distribution.

How it became deductible

  1. B LLC is a cash-basis S corporation. Interest is deductible when paid, as business interest under §163(a), on line 13 of the 1120-S. The small-business exception (receipts under $31M) keeps it outside the §163(j) limit.
  2. The deduction reduced ordinary income from what would have been about $100K to $69,133.
  3. That lower figure is the K-1, which lands on the owner's Schedule E and 1040. So the owner, not the company, got the tax benefit: at his 2025 position it protected roughly $6,800 of credit carryforward and saved about $2,000 of California tax, and it also lowered the QBI deduction base.

Why part of it is at risk

Interest follows the use of the money (Treas. Reg. §1.163-8T). The company borrowed $60.6K from Fundbox and Headway in 2025 while sending the owner $36.9K of new loans and $52.3K of distributions, more than its $69.1K of profit. To the extent the advances funded the owner's loan, the interest on that share is not business interest. For an S corporation it becomes investment interest passed through on the K-1, deductible by the owner only against his investment income ($7,230 in 2025), with the rest carried forward. Depending on how the cash actually moved, up to about $12K of the $30.9K deduction could be reclassified. There is a second mismatch: the company paid roughly 21% on its debt while charging the owner 0% on his.

4. If the loan is challenged

Courts weigh the same factors the IRS does: a written note, a stated rate, a repayment schedule, repayments actually made, collateral, the borrower's ability to repay, whether the company treated it consistently, and whether the amounts track with profits. A balance that only grows, with no interest and no payments, in a one-owner company paying itself $13,700, fails most of them. Possible outcomes on the full $125,100:

What protects loan treatment: a signed note, a stated rate at or above the applicable federal rate, a repayment schedule that is followed, collateral, and consistent treatment on the books. None of that is visible in the return.

5. Comparison: today's loan vs a split-dollar loan from a C corporation

The alternative you described is a loan-regime split-dollar arrangement (Treas. Reg. §1.7872-15): B becomes a C corporation, pays its own tax on earnings, and lends after-tax retained earnings to the shareholder, who uses them to pay premiums on a permanent life insurance policy he owns. The policy's cash value and death benefit are collaterally assigned to the corporation. The loan carries interest at the applicable federal rate, and is repaid from the cash value or the death benefit. Nothing is taxed to the shareholder when the money goes out, because it is a genuine, secured, interest-bearing loan.

How this applies to the existing $125,100

  • Converting to a C corporation is prospective. It does not change what the $125,100 already is. The IRS will still classify those past withdrawals as loan, distribution or wages based on how they were handled when made.
  • The existing balance could be novated into a documented note now: written terms, AFR interest, a repayment schedule, and collateral. Collateral is the part that is hardest in practice, because the owner would have to pledge something he owns, such as the rental property's equity or a new policy's cash value as it builds.
  • A split-dollar policy does not retire the old loan. It would be a second, separate loan for premiums. Using corporate cash to fund premiums while the first loan stays unpaid makes the first loan look worse, not better.
  • The clean sequence is: document or resolve the $125K first (repay, convert to salary over time, or declare a distribution in a year where the $59.9K capital-loss carryforward and the credits absorb most of the gain), then decide on entity type and whether a split-dollar plan fits a 31-year-old owner with a 10-year-plus horizon.
Bottom line of the comparison: the split-dollar structure has every feature the current loan lacks (after-tax source, note, interest, collateral, repayment path), which is exactly why it survives scrutiny. It pays for that with a 28% corporate tax up front, the loss of the QBI deduction, double taxation on anything eventually paid out, and slow access to cash. It is a long-horizon wealth-transfer tool, not a replacement for taking a salary.
