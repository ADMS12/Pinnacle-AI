Scenario 1: W-2 wages to owner and spouse, SEP-IRA, health insurance, personal life insurance

B LLC stays an S corporation. No further shareholder loans; the existing $125,100 loan is documented and repaid from salary. Revenue $600,000 with base-case costs. The owner and his wife are the only two employees; the spouse is paid $12,000. 2026 rules, married filing jointly.

The rules that drive the numbers

  • SEP-IRA. Employer-only contribution, up to 25% of W-2 wages, capped at $72,000 per person for 2026. The same percentage must apply to every eligible employee, so the spouse's SEP is 25% of her $12,000, or $3,000. Deductible by the S corp, not in anyone's W-2, no payroll tax on it. Can be funded as late as the extended return due date.
  • Wages. Every dollar of W-2 pays 15.3% in Social Security and Medicare up to the $184,500 wage base, 2.9% above it, half from the company and half from the employee. Wages must be reasonable for the work done; $12,000 for the spouse is easy to support with part-time bookkeeping, scheduling or marketing.
  • Health insurance. The S corp pays the family premium, adds it to the owner's W-2 box 1 (not subject to payroll tax), and the owner deducts it above the line. Net effect: fully deductible, no payroll tax. Assumed $18,000 a year.
  • Life insurance. An S corp cannot fund a policy for a more-than-2% shareholder on a tax-favored basis, so the owner buys the policy personally with after-tax money. Assumed $24,000 a year for a permanent policy with roughly $1.5M of death benefit on a healthy 31-year-old; an insurance illustration is needed to confirm. Not deductible.
  • Personal IRAs. Both spouses are covered by a workplace plan and joint income is above the phase-out, so traditional IRA contributions are not deductible. The $7,500 each can still go in as nondeductible contributions converted to Roth (backdoor Roth), shown as savings.
  • The old loan. Documented with a note at the applicable federal rate (about 4%, $5,004 a year on $125,100) and repaid at $25,000 a year from after-tax salary, about five years to clear. The interest is income to B LLC and flows back through the K-1.
  • QBI. The K-1 that remains gets the 20% deduction; joint income is under the $403,500 threshold so the wage cap does not apply. Wages never qualify.

    3. Protection and the loan

    What the family has at the end of year 1

    Death benefit and cash value depend on the policy chosen. A 20-year term policy of $1.5M would cost under $1,000 a year for this age and health, which covers the protection need immediately if the permanent policy is deferred.

    Why the loan has to move first

    • With salary at $140K the owner can carry a $25,000 annual repayment plus interest and still keep about $239K to spend. That turns a $125K exposure (capital gain or disguised wages, $28K to $35K of tax plus penalties) into an ordinary, documented note.
    • The repayments return cash to B LLC, which should go straight to the Fundbox and Headway balances. Those cost the company roughly 20% a year; every $25K repaid saves about $5K of interest annually, which raises next year's K-1.
    • An alternative is to declare part of the loan a distribution in a year where the $59,859 capital-loss carryforward absorbs the gain. That is faster but uses up the carryforward; it is worth comparing in the next round.
    Not solved by this scenario: the company still carries $171.5K of debt, and permanent life insurance is paid with after-tax dollars. Scenario 2 addresses both.
