Four risks the family is carrying today

1. The $125,100 shareholder loan

No note, no interest, no repayments, no collateral. The company has $7,505 of equity; without this receivable it is $125K underwater. If the IRS treats it as a distribution the owner owes capital gain tax on $117,595; if as wages, payroll tax on all of it. Exposure: $28K to $35K plus penalties. Lenders see it too.

2. No health insurance

A family with no coverage carries unlimited medical risk and gets no deduction. Coverage bought through the company is fully deductible and, in the right structure, carries no payroll tax.

3. No life protection

The household depends on one person's earnings from three businesses, with $171K of company debt and a new spouse. There is no death benefit in place. A $1.5M policy on a healthy 31-year-old is inexpensive relative to the income it replaces.

4. No retirement savings

Nothing has been set aside. At $600K of revenue the business can fund $38K to $48K a year in SEP-IRAs plus $15K in Roth IRAs, all of it tax-deferred or tax-free, without changing how the business runs.

The plan below addresses all four. Both structures end 2026 with the family insured, retirement funded, the loan on a repayment path, and the company paying down its 21% debt.

What makes a loan a loan

  • A signed note
  • Interest at or above the federal rate (about 4%)
  • A repayment schedule that is actually followed
  • Collateral
  • Consistent treatment on the books

None of these is visible in the 2025 return. Every year the balance grows without payments, the case for calling it a loan gets weaker.

Both plans convert this into a documented, interest-bearing note that is repaid, and send the repayments to the company's most expensive debt.

Scenario 1: W-2 wages, SEP-IRA, health through the S corp, personal life policy

How it works

  • Owner $140,000 and spouse $12,000 in W-2 wages. The S corp contributes 25% of both to SEP-IRAs ($38,000) and pays the $18,000 family health premium, both deductible.
  • Life insurance is bought personally: an S corp cannot fund a policy for its owner on a tax-favored basis. $24,000 a year after tax for about $1.5M of coverage.
  • The old loan goes on a 4% note and is repaid $25,000 a year from salary; B LLC uses the cash to pay down Fundbox and Headway.
  • Backdoor Roth IRAs, $7,500 each, from personal cash.
Simple, one entity, every risk addressed. The cost is that protection and the loan are paid with after-tax dollars.

Scenario 2: a sibling C corporation funds protection, retirement and the loan payoff

How it works

  • The owner holds B LLC and a new C corp side by side (a C corp cannot own an S corp). B LLC pays the C corp $300,000 a year for management, sales and admin services under a written agreement.
  • The C corp pays the owner $120,000 and the spouse $12,000, contributes $33,000 to SEP-IRAs, and provides the $18,000 health plan as a tax-free fringe benefit.
  • After 28% corporate tax it keeps $71,946. It lends $24,000 to the owner each year for the life policy (split-dollar, collateral assignment) and the rest to repay B LLC; the old loan is gone in year 3.
  • B LLC uses the repayments to retire its 21% debt, raising profit from year 2 on.
Protection and the loan payoff are funded with corporate dollars taxed at 28%, not personal dollars taxed at 33%. Money kept inside builds toward expansion and, after five years, possible QSBS treatment.

What the life insurance does for the family

Protection today

  • $1.5M death benefit replaces roughly 10 years of the household's current spendable income if the owner dies.
  • It clears the company's $171K of debt (the owner has almost certainly guaranteed the Fundbox and Headway balances) and the documented loans, so the spouse does not inherit a leveraged business and a note.
  • Income-tax free to the beneficiary under both scenarios.
  • Underwriting at 31 and in good health locks in the lowest lifetime cost; waiting makes every year more expensive and risks insurability.

Cash value over time (permanent policy)

  • Premiums of $24,000 a year build cash value that grows tax deferred. Early years are thin (cost of insurance and commissions); by year 10 cash value typically approaches cumulative premiums.
  • Policy loans against the cash value are tax free, a second source of retirement or emergency cash.
  • In Scenario 2 the corporation holds a collateral assignment: at death it is repaid first, the family receives the balance. In Scenario 1 the family receives all of it, but premiums came from after-tax income.
  • A $1.5M 20-year term policy costs under $1,000 a year and can be put in force now while the permanent policy is designed. Protection should not wait for the structure.
Scenario 1: family owns the policy outright. Cost $24,000 a year out of take-home, roughly $36,000 of pre-tax income at 33%.
Scenario 2: the C corp lends the premium from earnings taxed at 28%. The owner pays about 4% interest and nothing else; the corporation is made whole at death. The family keeps the policy and the rest of the benefit.

Death benefit and cash value figures are placeholders until an insurer's illustration is obtained. Disability coverage, not modeled here, is the other gap for a household dependent on one earner.

Recommendation and next steps

Start with Scenario 1 now, build toward Scenario 2

  • Immediately (this quarter): put the owner and spouse on payroll at $140K and $12K; enroll the family health plan through B LLC; bind a $1.5M term policy; sign a 4% note on the $125,100 loan and make the first payment; stop all undocumented withdrawals.
  • By year end: open SEP-IRAs and fund $38,000 for 2026 (can be paid until the extended due date); fund backdoor Roth IRAs; send every loan repayment to the Fundbox and Headway balances.
  • Then decide on the C corp: if the owner wants to retain earnings for acquisitions and fund the permanent policy with corporate dollars, form the sibling C corp for 2027 with a services agreement, and move payroll, SEP, health and the loan payoff into it as in Scenario 2.
