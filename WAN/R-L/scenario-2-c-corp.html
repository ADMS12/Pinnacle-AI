Scenario 2: A sibling C corporation billing B LLC $300,000, funding retirement, health, life insurance and the loan payoff from retained earnings

The owner holds two companies side by side: B LLC (still an S corporation) and a new C corporation. B LLC pays the C corp $300,000 a year for management, sales and administrative services. The C corp employs the owner ($120,000) and his wife ($12,000), funds SEP-IRAs and family health insurance, pays its own tax, and lends its after-tax retained earnings to the owner: first to fund a cash-value life insurance policy, then to repay the old $125,100 loan to B LLC, and afterwards to build an expansion reserve. Revenue $600,000 with base-case costs. 2026 rules, married filing jointly.
Why a sibling and not a parent. An S corporation cannot have a corporation as a shareholder. If the C corp owned B LLC, B's S election would end the same day and all of B's profit would be taxed twice. With the owner holding both companies directly, B LLC keeps its single level of tax and the 20% QBI deduction, and the C corp does what it is good at: retaining earnings at 28% and providing tax-free fringe benefits.

1. How the structure works

Money flow, year 1

The rules that drive the numbers

  • The $300,000 fee is deductible by B LLC and income to the C corp. It needs a services agreement, invoices, and real work performed through the C corp. The IRS can reallocate income between commonly controlled companies (§482) if the fee is not arm's length. B LLC pays the owner nothing directly.
  • Corporate tax. 21% federal, 8.84% California (deductible federally), about 28% combined. No QBI deduction on C corp income.
  • Wages and SEP. The C corp pays the owner $120,000 and the spouse $12,000, with payroll taxes, and contributes 25% of both to SEP-IRAs: $33,000, deductible by the C corp.
  • Health insurance is a tax-free fringe benefit for C corp employees: $18,000 deducted by the corporation, nothing added to the W-2. This is cleaner than the S corp treatment.
  • Retained earnings are lent, not distributed. Each loan is documented under a loan-regime split-dollar agreement (Treas. Reg. §1.7872-15) or a plain shareholder note: written terms, interest at the applicable federal rate (assumed 4%), and collateral. Loans are not income to the owner. The interest is taxable to the corporation and nondeductible for the owner.
  • Life insurance. The owner owns a permanent policy; the C corp lends the $24,000 premium each year and takes a collateral assignment of cash value and death benefit. At death the corporation recovers its loans plus interest from the death benefit and the family receives the rest income-tax free.
  • Dividends. None. Anything paid out as a dividend would be taxed again at 15% to 20% plus 3.8% NIIT plus California.

3. Five-year plan for the retained earnings

Premium first, then the old loan, then expansion

Assumes the C corp retains the same amount each year. Loans to the owner accrue 4% interest, payable annually.

  • The old $125,100 loan is cleared in year 3. The owner borrows from the C corp on a documented note and repays B LLC. B LLC uses the cash to retire the Fundbox and Headway balances and part of the vehicle debt. At roughly 20% average cost, every $48K returned saves about $10K of interest a year, which raises B LLC's K-1 from year 2 on (not modeled in the year-1 figures).
  • One exposure is replaced by a documented obligation. The owner ends year 3 owing the C corp about $197K at 4%, secured by the policy assignment and, ideally, a second collateral source such as the rental property's equity. That is a real debt with real interest, but it is not a tax time bomb.
  • Accumulated earnings tax. Lending to the shareholder is not a business need. The $250,000 lifetime credit covers the first three and a half years of retention at this pace; from year 4 the expansion reserve has to be documented (board minutes, acquisition plan) to avoid the 20% tax on further accumulations.

5. Risks and conditions

ItemWhat to watchLevel
Arm's-length feeThe $300K must be earned: real services, a contract, invoices, and no duplicate pay from B LLC for the same work. If the IRS reallocates the fee back to B LLC, the C corp deductions and retention disappear.Medium
Double tax on anything paid outEvery dollar that leaves the C corp as a dividend has already paid 28% and pays roughly 29% more. The structure only works for money that stays inside or goes out as salary, benefits or documented loans.Medium
Accumulated earnings tax20% on accumulations beyond reasonable needs once the $250K credit is used. Only the expansion reserve is a documented need.Medium
Loan disciplineThe whole structure rests on the loans being real: notes, AFR interest actually paid each year, collateral assignments filed with the insurer, consistent books. Skipping an interest payment reopens the recharacterization problem.High if ignored
Policy designEarly-year cash value is well below premiums paid. The corporation's collateral is thin for 5 to 10 years. Underwriting and an illustration are required before anything is signed.Medium
Reasonable compensation$120K is on the low side for someone running a $600K business through a second company. The IRS could call part of the retained earnings disguised wages; keep contemporaneous records of hours and duties.Medium
Cost and complexitySecond entity: separate books, payroll, returns (1120 and 100), licenses, insurance. $6,000 a year included.Low
