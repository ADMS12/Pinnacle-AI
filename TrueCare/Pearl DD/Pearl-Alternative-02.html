Pearl Hospice — Acquisition Analysis

2026-10-07 · Pinnacle Planning

Organizational, operational and financial review of Pearl Hospice LLC (Des Plaines, IL; CCN 141701) against the draft Management Agreement with Option to Purchase at $100,000 with 70% management-payment credit. Prepared for presentation to an alternative buyer. Draft, not legal advice.

Executive summary

Do not match the Jimenez proposal on its terms. Beat it with a cleaner structure that pays the seller more cash sooner, keeps the buyer off the hook for a three-year regulatory exposure, and prices the asset for what it is today: a licensed, Medicare-certified Illinois hospice with a collapsing census and a 100% prepayment review.

The $100,000 headline is not the real economics of the competing offer. Under it the buyer puts up a $50,000 deposit, collects 70% of net profit for 36 months as a management fee, and credits 70% of those fees against the price. On 2024 earnings that credit alone would exceed $100,000; on the best reading of 2025 it would consume most of the price. Either way the business largely buys itself for the deposit. The seller keeps 30% of profit, carries the license, the Medicare provider agreement and all survey and audit risk until late 2029, and is locked out of any other buyer until October 2030.

What the diligence shows:

  • The business peaked in 2024 and is shrinking fast. Revenue went from $143 (2022) to $200,453 (2023) to $854,658 (2024), then $806,043 in 2025 with a net loss of $125,721 per the tax return ($77,771 per the P&L in the data room). The July 2026 license renewal reports 2026 year-to-date Medicare income of $266,277 and an average census of 2.

  • A UPIC placed Pearl on 100% prepayment medical review on June 10, 2026. Every claim billed since then is held for documentation. Six ADRs totaling $36,094 are due November 8, 2026, with no response in the file. Cash collection has effectively stopped until these clear.

  • The owner stepped off payroll two days after the UPIC letter. Staff is down to seven people, three of them at 40 hours per two weeks. No physician, volunteer coordinator or bereavement staff appear on payroll.

  • The paper trail has gaps a buyer must close before signing. No CMS approval of the 2022 change of ownership is in the file, the Bill of Sale is unsigned by the buyer side, license numbers differ across filings, and no medical director agreement or liability insurance certificate was produced.

Our read on value: on a going-concern basis Pearl supported at most $51,000 of normalized annual earnings in 2025 on the tax-return figures, and that only if its management and consulting fees were owner-related, and that earnings base no longer exists in 2026. Today the asset is worth its licensed CCN plus clean receivables, less an unquantified Medicare overpayment exposure. A buyer who can stabilize operations, clear the prepayment review and rebuild census can create real value, but should pay for the shell now and for the recovery later.

Recommended counter: a 12-month management agreement at a fixed fee, an option fee of $25,000 to $50,000 credited in full, a purchase price of $100,000 to $150,000 set by an appraisal collar, cash at close, and a holdback for Medicare overpayments. Details in the counter-structure section.

Economics to the seller, illustrated. If Pearl earned the 2025 normalized figure of about $85,000 per year for three years, the Manager would collect about $178,500 in fees, the seller about $76,500. The 70% credit would reach $125,000, above the $100,000 price, so the seller would receive nothing further at closing and would have funded the buyer's purchase out of her own profit share. If instead earnings stay at the 2026 run rate, the seller collects little, still carries the license, and still cannot sell to anyone else.

Drafting defects to point out to the seller. The fee and price-credit clauses conflict; the deposit is undefined; entity names and license numbers are blank; the profit-share fee invites a federal fraud-and-abuse review; and the Exhibit A authorities amount to a de facto transfer of control that IDPH and CMS treat as a change of ownership or managing control requiring notice within 30 days (42 C.F.R. 424.516).

The 36-month rule. Since January 1, 2024, a hospice that undergoes a change in majority ownership within 36 months of its initial enrollment or its last such change must enroll as a new provider and be surveyed again (42 C.F.R. 424.550(b)). Pearl's last majority change was July 21, 2022, so the window closed in July 2025 and a sale now should not trigger it. Two caveats: the January 2026 PECOS filing changed "Individual Control," and if CMS treats that as a majority change the clock restarted; and the Jimenez structure, which hands bank, EIN and PECOS authority to a non-owner, could be characterized as an unreported change in managing control.

Gaps and inconsistencies to resolve before signing:

  • No CMS or Palmetto approval letter for the 2022 change of ownership is in the file. The document labeled as the CMS 855A response is a PECOS auto-receipt dated January 12, 2026 for a separate change-of-information filing, with no outcome shown.

  • The 2022 Form 855A checks "Seller/Former Owner" while the attached Bill of Sale shows VCG as buyer; pages 30 and 31 of the scanned form are missing; the Bill of Sale is unsigned by Eric Petersen (seller side) and by Vanessa Adraneda for VCG. The Membership Interest Purchase Agreement it references was not produced.

  • License number on the 855A (2003177, expired Mar 31, 2023) differs from the current license (2003258). Four different addresses appear across filings: Lombard, Crete, and two Des Plaines suites.

  • The 2026 IDPH application lists Vanessa Adraneda personally as 100% owner with VCG omitted and the hospice name blank on the ownership attachment, and is an unsigned renewal draft.

  • Pearl shares an address, branding and the owner's email domain with Devoted Healthcare, a separate home health, home care and staffing business. Any shared staff, leases or intercompany charges need to be separated at closing.

  • No certificate of liability or professional insurance, no medical director agreement, and no PTAN or EFT (CMS-588) documentation were produced.

  • Geoffrey Jimenez and Benjamin Cabrera appear in no corporate, payroll or vendor document. Their background, exclusion-list status and hospice experience are unknown.

Operational analysis

Pearl was a routine-home-care-only hospice averaging about 11 patients a day in 2025. In 2026 it is operating at a fraction of that, with a skeleton staff, under a federal integrity contractor's prepayment review.

Census and mix. The calendar 2025 Medicare PS&R shows 46 unduplicated beneficiaries, 186 claims and 4,084 routine home care days, which is an average daily census of 11.2. No continuous care, general inpatient or respite days were billed in 2025. The July 2026 license renewal reports 15 admissions year to date, a highest census of 6, lowest of 2 and average of 2.

The UPIC prepayment review. CoventBridge Group, the Unified Program Integrity Contractor for the Midwest, notified Pearl on June 10, 2026 that all claims billed after that date are subject to prepayment medical review (reference CSE-260323-00029). No reason, error rate or exit criteria are stated. Palmetto issued six ADRs on September 24, 2026, all due November 8, 2026, covering May through July 2026 service months and $36,094 in charges. The requested record set is the full hospice eligibility file: election statements, certifications with physician narratives, face-to-face attestations, IDG notes, plans of care and visit notes from start of care. No response, receipt or decision is in the file. The ADRs were mailed to the old Crete address, so Pearl's Medicare correspondence address may be stale.

A UPIC review of a small hospice almost always concerns terminal-prognosis eligibility and length of stay. Denials here would not just reduce revenue; they would establish an overpayment that can be extrapolated across prior claims and referred for revocation.

Financial analysis

Pearl earned real money for one year. It was dormant in 2022, grew to $855K of revenue and $132K of book profit in 2024, lost $78K on $801K in 2025, and is on pace for roughly $400K to $450K of revenue in 2026 before the prepayment review began holding claims.

Where the money went in 2025. Revenue was 99% Medicare. Against $801K of income, the four largest cost blocks were contract services $322K (40%), professional fees $172K (21%), payroll and payroll taxes $194K (24%) and medical records and supplies $81K (10%). Within those, $264,918 is booked as "Contract Services – Other" with no vendor breakdown, $80,300 as management fees and $67,833 as consulting, none of which trace to any contract in the file. In 2024 the equivalent unexplained line was "Contract Services – Office" at $210,350 plus $83,500 of consulting.

Even the generous 2025 figure rests on a census that no longer exists. At the 2026 run rate of about 2 to 6 patients, revenue cannot cover the current $148K annualized payroll plus the $18K InvaServ minimum, pharmacy, supplies and a medical director. The business is losing cash today.

Unit economics check. 2025 Medicare revenue of $796,656 over 4,084 routine home care days is $195 per day, consistent with the FY2025 Illinois RHC rate net of the 61-day step-down and sequestration, so revenue is not overstated. The P&L Medicare revenue of $796,656 ties within 5% to the PS&R net reimbursement of $760,547; the difference is timing and the $4,550 of private insurance.

Cash conversion under prepayment review. From June 10, 2026 every claim is held until the UPIC reviews a full medical record. Typical turnaround is 60 to 90 days after documentation is received, and denials are common in the first cycles. A buyer should assume the receivable on the books at closing is collectible at a substantial discount and that no Medicare cash arrives for the first quarter of ownership.

Other facts from the returns a buyer must weigh:

  • Ownership differs from the Medicare filings. All three years' K-1s show Vanessa Adraneda holding 90% directly and VCG Healthcare, Inc. 10%. The 2022 Form 855A reports VCG at 100% with Adraneda as 100% indirect owner, and the 2026 IDPH application lists Adraneda at 100%.   • Cash is nearly gone. Schedule L cash fell from $128,680 at the start of 2025 to $5,104 at year end. Accounts receivable is not carried on the balance sheet (cash-basis books), so the collectible Medicare AR is unknown.

  • Owner loans, not distributions. No partner distributions are reported in any year, yet loans from partners moved from $169,080 to $82,029 and the 2025 balance sheet carries a $96,136 asset titled "Loans from (to) Owners" against a $178,165 liability "Loans from (to) Partners." Money has been moving between the company and its owner outside the distribution line.

  • Related-party balances. Due to or from Devoted Health Care, Archos, Archos Staffing, "Buyer," "Seller's account," Distinct, Cardinal ($12,800), Aishling ($16,000) and an "Unknown Source" ($2,392) appear on the balance sheet. None are documented.

  • Goodwill. $178,022 of intangible asset from the 2022 purchase, amortized at $11,869 a year, net $136,482 at the end of 2025. This is the price the current owner paid for the business.

  • Compliance posture. The partner signature line is blank on all three Forms 1065 and on the 2023 and 2025 Illinois returns; each return was prepared four to five months after the March due date with no extension in the file; the 2025 Schedule L does not balance at the beginning of the year.

For the valuation that follows, the tax-return numbers govern. On that basis the 2025 normalized EBITDA is a loss of about $97,000 before adding back management and consulting fees, and about $51,000 of profit after.

Valuation

Pearl is worth the value of its Medicare-certified license plus clean receivables today, with upside only if a buyer can clear the prepayment review and rebuild census. The $100,000 headline price is defensible for the shell; the competing proposal's problem is not the number but that the seller funds it and waits three years for it.

Recommended counter-structure

Beat the competing offer on certainty and timing, not on headline price. The seller's real alternatives are 30% of a shrinking profit for three years, or cash now and a clean exit. The buyer's real risk is the overpayment exposure, which a holdback handles better than a discount.

What to say to the seller. The Jimenez offer gives her 30% of whatever profit a stranger reports, no cash at closing because the credit consumes the price, and four years of license and audit liability. This counter gives her an option fee now, a fixed fee stream, a price at least equal to the competing headline, and an exit in 12 to 18 months with the regulatory risk handled by a holdback instead of an open-ended indemnity.

What the buyer must be willing to do. Fund roughly $60,000 to $100,000 of working capital and restaffing in the first six months on top of the price, personally lead the ADR responses, and accept that the business may need to be rebuilt almost from zero if the UPIC review goes badly.

For the alternative buyer

  • Are you buying a going concern or a licensed shell? The answer sets whether the price is $100,000 flat or an appraisal collar.

  • Can you place a licensed administrator and a director of nursing on day one? Without them the management term cannot stabilize the hospice.

  • How much working capital can you commit beyond the price? Our estimate is $60,000 to $100,000 for the first six months.

  • Do you want receivables at all? If not, the price is cleaner and the seller keeps collection risk on pre-closing claims.

  • Is a 12-month term acceptable, or do you need the full 36 months the seller has already seen?
