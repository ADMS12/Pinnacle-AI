Analysis of the Jimenez Management Agreement with Option to Purchase

Prepared October 7, 2026 · Draft agreement dated October 5, 2026 · Pinnacle Planning

The offer is a 36-month management arrangement, not a sale. The Manager takes 70% of profit and operating control now, credits 70% of what he collects against a $100,000 price, and decides in 2029 whether to buy. On any year in which Pearl earns what it earned in 2024 or 2025, the credit consumes most or all of the price, so the seller funds the buyer's purchase out of her own profit share while keeping the license, the Medicare provider agreement and every audit and survey liability until closing.

Where the seller's downside sits

  1. She pays for the sale. In Scenarios A and B the credit exceeds the price, so no cash changes hands at closing. Her entire proceeds are her 30% profit share plus the deposit. In Scenario A the Manager collects $511,640 in fees and nets $461,640 for a business whose stated price is $100,000.
  2. Her upside is capped and her downside is not. If the hospice recovers, 70% of the recovery goes to the Manager and the price does not move. If it fails, the expenses, the license, the Medicare overpayment exposure and the UPIC prepayment review stay with her. The Manager is expressly not liable for any expense (Sec. 7).
  3. The Manager can walk; she cannot. The option is a right, not an obligation. After collecting three years of fees he may decline to buy. She, meanwhile, cannot sell or negotiate with anyone until October 2030, and even terminating the management does not kill the option (Sec. 23).
  4. The books that set the fee are in the Manager's hands. "Net profit" is undefined, and Exhibit A gives the Manager's representative signing authority on the bank account and the enrollment records. There is no audit right, no accounting standard and no cap on what the Manager may charge the hospice before profit is struck.
  5. The Exhibit B credit wording is a trap. "70% of Reimbursement" would credit 70% of Medicare receipts rather than 70% of fees. If that language survives into the signed version, the price is extinguished within a quarter.
  6. Regulatory exposure attaches to her license, not his. A profit-share management fee paid to a party doing marketing and referral-source work is the fact pattern the OIG has repeatedly flagged under the Anti-Kickback Statute. The licensee, not the manager, answers to IDPH and CMS. Handing PECOS and bank authority to a non-owner without a change-of-ownership filing adds a reporting failure on top of that.
  7. The price is already below what she paid. The 2022 purchase booked $178,022 of goodwill, net $136,482 at the end of 2025. A $100,000 price three years from now, funded by her own profit share, recovers none of it.
  8. Timing works against her. The hospice is under 100% prepayment review as of June 10, 2026 with six ADRs due November 8, 2026. For the next 36 months the Manager will be the one deciding how, and whether, those are answered, while the overpayment liability remains hers under Exhibit A.

What the offer is worth to each side

To the Manager it is a free option on a licensed Medicare hospice, financed by the hospice's own cash flow, with a three-year look before any commitment. To the seller it is a 30% minority income stream on a business she still owns and is still liable for, followed by a closing in which she is paid, at most, whatever is left of $100,000 after the credit. On every earnings level Pearl has actually recorded, that remainder is between $0 and $25,109.

Bottom line. The $100,000 headline is not the problem; the mechanics are. Any competing offer that pays an option fee in cash now, uses a fixed management fee instead of a profit share, keeps the credit to the option fee, and closes in 12 to 18 months with a holdback for Medicare exposure beats this draft for the seller at the same headline price.
